 When soil is wet, water flow to roots is not limited by soil hydraulic properties, making plant hydraulic traits the primary drivers of variation in soil–plant water
 flow. Under most atmospheric conditions when soils are wet, plant hydraulic traits enable plants to fully transpire, meaning that the actual transpiration rate
 approaches the potential transpiration. We refer to this as Phase I, during which the gradient between soil and leaf water potential equals the transpiration
 rate E divided by the (maximum) plant hydraulic conductance (KP). This simplified model ignores short-term (within and across days) variability in
 evaporative demand to focus on long-term processes that are relevant as drought progressively worsens. It is based on a relatively constant transpiration rate
 during this phase, assuming maximal stomatal conductance (gmax) and constant evaporative demand (vapor pressure deficit, VPD). Under these conditions,
 the majority of water used by the plant is extracted from soil and there is limited use of water resources stored within the plant.
     As the soil dries and can no longer match the full transpirational demand of the plant, the leaf water potential (Ψleaf) progressively declines until stomata
 start to close and stomatal conductance (gs) decreases. We refer to this critical leaf water potential as Ψ_crit1. As Ψleaf and gs continue to decline, a second
 threshold is reached – Ψ_crit2 – resulting in full stomatal closure and a minimum stomatal conductance (gmin, i.e. water loss through closed stomata and the
 cuticle). We refer to this phase, during which gs and E decline from their maximum to minimum values as Phase II. During Phase II, growth continues,
 although at a limited rate, with plants relying on any remaining extractable soil water resources and using increasingly more of their internally stored water
 resources. Positive cell turgor is achieved via osmotic adjustment, while photosynthesis, carbon partitioning (e.g. belowground vs aboveground), and
 growth proceed at reduced rates.
     During the following phase (Phase III), stomata remain closed (gmin) and plants continue to lose water through cuticular conductance and stomatal
 leakage. At this stage, soil water availability has declined to the point where plants can no longer extract any water and they must rely on internally stored
 water released from capacitance tissues. Growth is fully stopped and the plant enters a survival mode, eventually leading to plant mortality at Ψ_crit3.
     The duration of the three phases depends on: (1) the rate of water transpired through plants (Emax and Emin), which depends on plant hydraulic
 conductance (KP), maximal stomatal conductance (gmax), evaporative demand (leaf-to-atmosphere VPD), and the transpiring surface area of the plant; (2)
 volume of available water in soil and plant tissues, determined by soil depth, root depth/distribution, soil hydraulic properties, plant tissue capacitance and
 water storage capacity, and external inputs (e.g. rain/irrigation); and (3) leaf water potential thresholds (Ψ_crit1, Ψ_crit2, and Ψ_crit3), resulting in different
 timing for stomatal closure and plant death.
     Plants with high stomatal conductance (gmax), KP, and/or with a large leaf area (conceivably also having high growth potential) should have short Phases I
 and II due to using their available water sources quickly. By contrast, plants with lower stomatal conductance, smaller leaf area, larger water storage, or
 access to larger soil water reservoir, should have longer Phases I and II due to using their water sources more slowly. Phases I and II also lengthen under
 conditions of low evaporative demand (low radiation, cool temperatures, high relative humidity, and limited wind).
     Plants that have high Emax but low KP should initiate stomatal closure in relatively wetter soils and have a relatively high (less negative) Ψ_crit1 and a
 shorter Phase I; therefore, ‘saving’ water in soil to be used later, assuming it does not evaporate or get used by competitors. The difference between Ψleaf at
 initial and final stomatal closure impacts the duration of Phase II, together with the volumes of water available in soil and in the plant between the respective
 potentials. In general, the duration of Phases I and II depends on the water economic balance between fluxes and how plants regulate them, which are
 related to critical thresholds of Ψleaf at the start and end of stomatal closure, and on water storage pools (in soil and plants).
     The duration of Phase III is solely controlled by plant traits, such as tissue capacitance, tissue water volume, gmin and tolerance to negative water
 potentials, for example the water potential resulting in plant death. Under some circumstances (high radiative losses at nighttime, vapor pressure close to
 dew point, and low wind speed), foliar water uptake may also be a substantial contributor to the water balance of a plant during Phase III (Dawson &
 Goldsmith, 2018; Fradera-Soler et al., 2024).

 Box 2 Modelling water use over time.

 We present a minimal mechanistic model to demonstrate the effects of plant traits on the time required to reach the three critical physiological thresholds
 illustrated in Box 1. Our intent with this idealized model is to test the claims we make in Box 1 using measured data from a plant community that includes
 species exhibiting several of the water use traits and strategies discussed above. Since our approach uses a proof-of-principle framework of minimalistic
 complexity, our motivation is not to examine the entire set of plant, soil, and climate characteristics, but rather to constrain the model to a small set of relevant
 traits directly affecting the water use and survival trade-off presented in Box 1. Toward this goal, we have simplified the model to include seven plant traits
 influencing water use: (1) maximum stomatal conductance (gmax), (2) minimum stomatal conductance (gmin), (3) leaf area fraction (Aleaf; leaf area relative to
 total dry plant mass), (4) leaf water potential initiating stomatal closure (Ψ_crit1), (5) leaf water potential resulting in full stomatal closure (Ψ_crit2), (6) the
 leaf water potential resulting in plant death (Ψ_crit3), assumed equal to the leaf water potential resulting in an 88% reduction of hydraulic conductance
 (P88), and (7) the shoot usable water fraction between full hydration and P88 (Eqns 1–3).
     The model was parameterized using data from a dry-down experiment undertaken at an Australian wet forest site and desert shrubland site (Gleason
 et al., 2014). The woody perennial species measured in this study were placed in groups exhibiting either high embolism resistance (P88 < 9.0 MPa; Acacia
 aneura, Casuarina pauper), high succulence/capacitance (usable water fractions >0.70 g g1 between full hydration and P88; Eremophila longifolia and
 Sida petrophila), or high gas exchange rates (gmax > 300 mmol m2 s1; Banksia serrata and Eucalyptus haemastoma), and are hereafter referred to as
 ‘Embolism resistant perennials’, ‘succulent perennials’, and ‘fast-growing perennials’, respectively. Data from two crop species (Zea mays and Helianthus
 annuus; Cardoso et al., 2018; Allen et al., 2025) and seven resurrection species (Barbacenia sp and Vellozia sp; Alcantara et al., 2015) were added to our
 analyses and are referred to as ‘annual crops’ and ‘resurrection perennials’, respectively.
     Shoot size varied across species and replicates in the dry-down experiment and ranged from 38 g to 116 g of oven-dried mass. Therefore, initial water
 volumes in plant tissue were scaled to a common dry mass of 100 g. To facilitate the comparison of trait effects, we assigned a constant rhizosphere volume
 of 0.36 m3 for all species and used the Brooks–Corey equation (Brooks & Corey, 1964) to convert between soil water content and soil water potential for a
 loam soil. Similarly, we assumed the leaf water potentials resulting in initial (1.4 MPa) and full stomatal closure (2.5 MPa; Martin-StPaul et al., 2017)
 were the same across species. Although this is an oversimplification that is the xylem water potential resulting in full stomatal closure varies from c. 1.0 MPa
 to 3.5 MPa across species (Martin-StPaul et al., 2017), this assumption was necessary because these data were not available for the species examined, and
 this simplification allowed for a better comparison of the modelled traits under similar plant and soil conditions (Fig. 3). As such, these results should be
 interpreted as a conceptual demonstration only, particularly when plant size, soil rhizosphere dimensions, and stomatal regulation vary widely from our
 assumptions. To compare the efficacy of each trait to influence the time of each drought phase, we carried out a second simulation where we allowed each
 trait to vary between its measured minimum and maximum values while holding the other traits at a constant across-species mean value (Fig. 4). During this

 Box 3 An ideal experiment to test the water use economics framework.

 To evaluate our unified framework of plant water use economics, we propose an ‘ideal experiment’ designed to quantify the duration and total water use of
 each phase and identify tipping points that govern transitions between them. While findings from controlled glasshouse experiments have limitations when
 extrapolated to natural field conditions, we focus here on a glasshouse-based design for the sake of simplicity and experimental tractability. Ideally, the
 experiment should be carried out during the season when plants commonly experience drought in natural conditions to test the role of seasonal phenological
 adaptations.
    We recommend sourcing seeds from a single population of the target species to minimize genetic variability, although the genetic constraints on the
 variability of these traits may be of particular interest for future studies. For soil, a well-draining potting mix supplemented with essential macro- and
 micronutrients is ideal. A similar quantity of dry substrate allows monitoring soil water moisture by gravimetry after irrigation cessation. Germination
 protocols will likely vary by species due to wide-ranging dormancy mechanisms.
    Once germinated, transplant at least three seedlings in appropriately sized pots (> 3.8 l; dependent on species) and maintain them under well-water
 conditions – watering to saturation at least every other day to maintain soil volumetric water content > 20%. Vapor pressure deficit should be kept within a
 nonstressful range (1–1.5 kPa). This establishment period should continue for at least 6 wk or until individuals are mature. The specific duration will vary by
 species, but trait measurements will require at least three to five fully expanded leaves per plant. For trait measurements, see Table 1.
    After the establishment period, the plants have entered Phase I. A subset of pots should be allocated to nondestructive trait measurements, including
 light-saturated photosynthetic rate, leaf and stem hydraulic conductance, and canopy architecture (height, number of leaves, leaf angle, and total leaf area).
 These same individuals can then be destructively harvested to assess leaf economic traits (specific leaf area (SLA) and leaf nitrogen content), biomass
 allocation (i.e. root : shoot ratio, Huber value), hydraulic threshold traits (P50, TLP, cell wall elasticity, and capacitance at full turgor), anatomical
 characteristics (stomatal density, xylem density, and diameter), and biochemical compounds in mature tissues and meristems.
    Following Phase I destructive trait characterization, a separate subset of plants should be subjected to a dry-down period to induce Phase II. Half of these
 pots should be randomly assigned for destructive measurements during Phase II, while the remaining half proceed to Phase III. A ‘priming drought’ (initial
 brief dehydration followed by rehydrating) may be necessary to elicit a more ecologically relevant physiological response and should be considered based on
 the species/region. During Phase II, we recommend monitoring soil moisture or matric potential, midday leaf water potential, and stomatal conductance on
 a daily basis, depending on the rate of soil dry down. Destructive measurements during this phase can include osmotic adjustment and concentrations of
 osmolytes in both mature tissues and meristems, and biomass production (root mass ratio or fine root:leaf area ratio), as well as leaf area dynamics. These
 traits can be quantitatively compared with Phase I vulnerability curves to assess potential shifts in growth maintenance strategies.
    Phase III begins when full stomatal closure occurs. At this stage, successive re-watering as soil moisture decreases (with proper monitoring of soil water
 content) of a subset of the pots not previously harvested allows for assessment of drought survival, that is the ability to produce new leaves following re-
 watering. A subset of individuals within a given pot can be sampled for traits that may be indicative of their survival potential, including meristematic and
 whole-plant water content, gmin, leaf area dynamics, native xylem embolism, leaf Chl content (senescence), root viability, membrane stability, and osmolyte
 concentrations in any remaining green tissue.
    By subjecting plants to this controlled, phased drought progression – paired with comprehensive trait assessments, in particular the water use and
 duration of each phase – a species position within the unified framework of plant water economics can be determined. Furthermore, potential ecological
 trade-offs between Phases I, II, and III can be identified and then further tested in field conditions with more complex biotic and abiotic interactions.
