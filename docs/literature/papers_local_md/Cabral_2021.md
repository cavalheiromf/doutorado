Atlantic Forest Inselbergs (AFI) and Campos Rupestres (CR) are mountains and highlands of eastern South
America, relatively poorly studied and highly threatened, which display extraordinary levels of plant endemism
and richness. In spite of their geographical and environmental differences, the origin of the flora of CR and AFI
are likely linked to each other, because several plant clades are distributed across both ecosystems. In addition to
these studies, little has been investigated about the historical biogeographical connections between AFI and CR
and most evolutionary studies are restricted to CR. Barbacenia (Velloziaceae) is widely spread and nearly
endemic to the AFI and CR outcrops and thus represent an ideal system to study the biogeographical connections
between CR and AFI. Besides, given the remarkable diversity of Barbacenia in CR compared to AFI, it appears that
different factors were important drivers in the diversification of Barbacenia lineages, likely leading to different
patterns of morphological diversification. Here, we integrate phylogenetic, biogeographic and morphological
approaches to: (i) address whether AFI species of Barbacenia are monophyletic and thus a single colonization of
AFI can be inferred; (ii) understand the timing and geographical origin of CR and AFI clades; (iii) compare
morphological diversity between Barbacenia from AFI and CR under the hypothesis that these two systems have
experienced similar levels of morphological diversification during their evolutionary history. To this end, we
presented a phylogeny inferred using plastid (atpB-rbcL, trnH-psbA and trnL-trnF) and nuclear (ITS) markers and a
complete sampling of AFI Barbacenia, estimated divergence times, reconstructed the ancestral areas of Barbacenia
clades and compared their morphological diversity based on a dataset of 16 characters. Our results provided
evidence for a diversification of Barbacenia from the Middle Miocene to Pleistocene, as suggested in previous
studies. We suggest that stepping-stone dispersal across mountaintops in interplay with paleovegetation dy­
namics during the global Miocene cooling and Pleistocene climatic oscillations may played an important role in
the range expansion of modern AFI Barbacenia lineages. Finally, our results also showed a significant differences
in morphological diversity between AFI and CR clades, suggesting a long-term morphological stasis in AFI
species.

1. Introduction
Mountain environments harbor high levels of species richness and
endemism, especially in the tropics (Rahbek et al., 2019). The origin of
such high diversity has intrigued scientists since the times of Humboldt.
In the Neotropics, most attention has focused on explaining the origin of
Andean diversity (Gentry, 1982; Hoorn et al., 2010; Luebert and Wei­
gend, 2014). However, the eastern Brazilian highlands have received

comparatively less attention in spite of their high levels of plant species
richness and endemism (Porembski et al., 1998; Porembski, 2007; Saf­
ford and Martinelli, 2000; de Paula et al., 2016; Fernandes, 2016). In
particular, Campos Rupestres (CR, Fig. 1b) and Atlantic Forest In­
selbergs (AFI, or Inselbergs s.l., Safford and Martinelli, 2000; Fig. 1c-f)
concentrate ca. 3400 endemic species of Angiosperms, which represent
ca. 10% of the Brazilian endemic diversity (FDB, 2020). These areas also
comprise centers of diversity for desiccation-tolerant vascular plants

Although both AFI and CR have similar physiognomies (i.e., shrub­
lands, grasslands and rocky outcrops), they are geographically and
environmentally different, AFI comprising granite and gneiss outcrops
distributed across the Brazilian Serra da Mantiqueira and Serra do Mar,
while CR being formed of quartzite, ironstone and sandstone substrates
largely occupying the Espinhaço Range (Vasconcelos, 2011). In spite of
these differences, the origin of the flora of CR and AFI are likely linked to
each other, because several plant clades are distributed across both
ecosystems, as inferred from phylogenetic studies in Apocynaceae (e.g.,
Simões et al., 2006), Asteraceae (e.g., Heiden et al., 2019), Bromeliaceae
(e.g., Givnish et al., 2011), Eriocaulaceae (e.g., Trovó et al., 2013),
Orchidaceae (e.g., Batista et al., 2013) and Velloziaceae (Alcantara
et al., 2018). In addition to these studies, little has been investigated
about the historical biogeographical connections between AFI and CR
and most evolutionary studies are restricted to CR (see below).
Geological evidence suggests that AFI and CR have been a scenario of
ancient geological changes. For the Serra da Mantiqueira and Serra do
Mar, uplift was associated with the break-up of west Gondwana and the
later elevation of the eastern Brazilian margin during Early Cretaceous
(Gontijo-Pascutti et al., 2012; Varajão and Alkmim, 2015). However, the
current surface exposure and shaping of many AFI likely began after
erosion processes under arid conditions of the Neogene (Varajão and
Alkmim, 2015; Vieira and Gramani, 2015). For the Espinhaço Range,
orogeny occurred even earlier, primarily associated with the origin of
Gondwana during the Precambrian (Hoppe, 1980; Alkmin, 2012). Even
so, there is evidence that plant colonization and diversification in CR
was rapid and as recent as in mountains of more recent uplift, such as the

Andes (Rapini et al., 2020; cf., Luebert and Weigend, 2014; Vasconcelos
et al., 2020). Hence, contrary to other mountain systems, its orogeny has
probably contributed little to the assembly of its unique and excep­
tionally rich biota which was largely originated from the Neogene. This
has also been shown in a number of plant clades comprising AFI species,
such as in Alstroemeriaceae (Alstroemeria and Bomarea; Chacón et al.,
2012), Bromeliaceae (Aechmea, Pitcairnia and Tillandsia; Givnish et al.,
2011), Gesneriaceae (Sinningia and Vanhouttea; Perret et al., 2012),
Lentibulariaceae (Utricularia; Silva et al., 2018), Malpighiaceae (Amor­
imia; Almeida et al., 2018) and Melastomataceae (Leandra; Reginato and
Michelangeli, 2019). The study of Vasconcelos et al. (2020) compiled
and compared nearly all divergence time studies so far conducted on CR
plant lineages and concludes that their diversification goes back to a
mosaic of idiosyncratic, lineage-specific evolutionary histories, with the
bulk of lineages originating either during the Miocene or Pliocene. Cli­
matic shifts have been invoked to explain the origin and diversification
of these groups in CR, especially the global cooling trend since the
Middle Miocene (Zachos et al., 2008) may have caused a wide expansion
of open habitats and was concomitant with the diversification of many
dry-specialized plant lineages (Alcantara et al., 2018; Antonelli et al.,
2010; Arakaki et al., 2011; Lavor et al., 2019; Perret et al., 2012).
Thereafter, high climatic instability during Pliocene-Pleistocene (last c.
5 million years), might have led to diversification due to range fluctu­
ations and changing connectivity between (mountain) populations
(“flickering connectivity”, Flantua et al., 2019, see also Rahbek et al.,
2019, Vasconcelos et al., 2020, but see Rapini et al., 2020 for an alter­
native explanation).

and AFI (Mello-Silva in FDB, 2020), and thus represent an ideal system

across a clade dominated by CR species. This suggests that AFI had been
colonized several times independently. Conversely, Barbacenia was
shown to form two sister clades largely endemic to CR and AFI, sug­
gesting two separate radiations on these areas. However, sampling of
Barbacenia AFI species in Alcantara et al. (2018) was limited and thus a
phylogeny with all species is required to test its monophyly and evaluate
the hypothesis of a single colonization of AFI by Barbacenia.
Barbacenia is widely spread and nearly endemic to the AFI and CR
outcrops (Mello-Silva in FDB, 2020), being one of many examples of
disjunct lineages between these areas (Fiaschi and Pirani, 2009; Alcan­
tara et al., 2018). The genus comprises heliophyte plants (Smith, 1962)
associated with extensive dry seasons and specific substrates (Behnke
et al., 2013; Porembski, 2007). Alcantara et al. (2018) suggested that the
acquisition of aquiferous parenchyma between bundles in the leaves
might had improved its survival chances in the cold and arid Miocene
conditions, thus leading to expansion of populations and ensuing
accelerated diversification. However, given the environmental differ­
ences and the remarkable diversity of CR Barbacenia compared to AFI
(Mello-Silva in FDB, 2020), it appears that different factors were
important drivers in the diversification of these sister clades as well as in
maintaining AFI and CR rocky-specific floras, likely leading to different
patterns of morphological diversification. Environmental heterogeneity
is thought to play a relevant role in speciation and adaptation (Stein
et al., 2014). Such factor can provide ecological opportunity for lineages
with high rates of phenotypic evolution, which therefore could
specialize on different resources and coexist within local assemblages
(Rabosky and Adams, 2012) hence leading to adaptive diversification.
Here, we integrate phylogenetic, biogeographic and morphological
approaches to address three questions about Brazilian AFI and CR evo­
lution using Barbacenia as a study case. First, we assess whether AFI
species of Barbacenia are monophyletic and thus a single colonization of
AFI can be inferred. Second, we analyze the historical biogeography of
Barbacenia to understand the timing and geographical origin of CR and
AFI clades. Third, we compare morphological diversity between Bar­
bacenia from AFI and CR under the hypothesis that these two systems
have experienced similar levels of morphological diversification during
their evolutionary history. To this end, we presented a phylogeny
inferred using plastid (atpB-rbcL, trnH-psbA and trnL-trnF) and nuclear
(ITS) markers and a complete sampling of AFI Barbacenia and its disjunct
infra-specific population (Fig. 1a, g-k), we estimated divergence times,
reconstructed the ancestral areas of Barbacenia clades and compared
their morphological diversity based on a dataset of 16 characters. We
expect that the AFI and CR Barbacenia species emerged during the
Miocene climatic cooling, as previously suggested (~20 million years,
Alcantara et al., 2018). We also expect that most colonization events
within the AFI took place during the climatically dynamic PlioPleistocene period, consistent with previous findings in CR areas (Vas­
concelos et al., 2020). Finally, since the distribution of Barbacenia in
both CR and AFI spans similar latitudinal ranges (Mello-Silva in FDB,
2020) and are both tropical mountain areas forming archipelago-like
systems (Vasconcelos, 2011; Alves et al., 2014), we expect morpholog­
ical diversity to be comparable between clades of Barbacenia in AFI and
CR when corrected by species richness.

2. Material and methods
2.1. Taxon sampling
One population from each type-locality of 10 out of 12 AFI Barba­
cenia species was sampled in the field (see Supplementary data 1 for
voucher information). In addition, a second specimen was collected for
the 10 species with disjunct distribution between inselbergs, with the
exception of B. tomentosa. Together with the remaining two species (i.e.,
B. tomentosa and B. mantiqueirae) gathered from (Alcantara et al., 2018),
this sampling comprised all species of Barbacenia with known occur­
rence for AFI areas. Fresh samples were collected and pressed according
to standard methods and deposited in the CESJ and SPF collections.
Leaves were preserved in silica gel for DNA extraction; for anatomical
and morphological analysis, leaves, pedicels, fruits and flowers were
kept in alcohol 70%. Phylogenetic analysis were based on the 20 sam­
ples obtained in the present study and those gathered from Alcantara
et al. (2018, Supplementary data 2). Eighty new sequences for ten spe­
cies were newly generated for this study and 656 sequences for 149
species were obtained from Alcantara et al. (2018). Complete matrix was
composed by a total of 158 species of Velloziaceae, including Acan­
thochlamys (1 spp.), Barbacenia (51 spp.), Barbaceniopsis (4 spp.), Vellozia
(77 spp.), Xerophyta (19 spp.), and 6 outgroup species. Therefore, total
sampling comprised 100% of the currently known diversity of Acan­
thochlamys, 51% of Barbacenia, 100% of Barbaceniopsis, 64% of Vellozia,
and 61% of Xerophyta. Outgroup taxa comprised other Pandanales
(Cyclanthus bipartitus Poit. ex A.Rich., Pandanus pygmaeus Thouars, and
Thoracocarpus bissectus (Vell.) Harling) and Dioscoreales species (Aletris
farinosa L., Dioscorea membranacea Pierre ex Prain & Burkill, and Tacca
chantrieri André), the latter being the sister order to Pandanales (sensu
APG IV, 2016).
2.2. DNA sequencing
Total genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue using the Nucle­
ospin II kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manu­
facturer’s protocol. DNA quantification was performed via gel
electrophoresis using 1% agarose gels and using a Nanodrop spectro­
photometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). For each
sample, we amplified the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and the
chloroplast regions atpB-rbcL, trnH-psbA and trnL-trnF (information on
PCR conditions available in Supplementary data 3). The choice of mo­
lecular markers was based on previous studies, which showed that these
regions provide a useful phylogenetic signal for Velloziaceae, either at
intergeneric or infrageneric levels (Alcantara et al., 2018; Behnke et al.,
2000; Mello-Silva et al., 2011; Salatino et al., 2001). Sequencing re­
actions were performed using the BigDye Terminator kit 3.1 (Applied
Biosystems, Foster, CA, USA). The purified sequencing products were
run on an ABI3130XL sequencer (Applied Biosystems) in the GaTE
Laboratory, Institute of Biosciences, University of São Paulo. Raw se­
quences were imported and assembled using Geneious v.9 (Kearse et al.,
2012). Sequence files were edited and manually aligned separately for
each region using PhyDE v.0.9971 (Müller et al., 2005). The ITS region
was also aligned using MAFFT v.7.22 (Katoh et al., 2002). Voucher in­
formation and GenBank accession numbers are listed in Supplementary
data 1.

simulated dataset, the ratios MCR/MAFI and DAFI/DCR were calculated as
described above. To depict these results, we also represented the data on
the first two principal coordinates using the phylomorphospace
approach (Sidlauskas, 2008) implemented in the R-package phytools

4.1. Middle Miocene origin of the two major lineages of Barbacenia
The results obtained in the present study provide evidence for a
diversification of Barbacenia from the Middle Miocene to the Pleisto­
cene. This is consistent with previous studies that have estimated
divergence times in Neotropical Velloziaceae (Alcantara et al. 2018;

Miocene, forming two endemic lineages to each of these regions. The
Serra da Mantiqueira lineage comprised four clades originated at
different times since the Late Miocene. These clades have both endemic
and non-endemic members to these AFI areas, suggesting two inde­
pendent dispersal events back into Serra do Mar (clade 5 and B. rogieri)
and one into Chapada Diamantina (Barbacenia sp. 1).
The largest Barbacenia clade (clades 7–10) originated in Campos
Rupestres from the Southern Espinhaço. This region is the core area of
Campos Rupestres (Conceição et al., 2016), embedded in domains of
which most are Cerrado (sensu Colli-Silva et al., 2019). Although the
Cerrado flora was assembled mostly over the past 4–5 Myr (Simon et al.,
2009), our results, as well previous studies (e.g., Antonelli et al., 2010,
Vasconcelos et al., 2020), suggest older ages for Southern Espinhaço
flora than those of most Cerrado lineages. This clade of Barbacenia
diversified towards the Middle/Late Miocene boundary and remained
restricted to this area until today, with the exception of single dispersals
into Chapada Diamantina (B. contasana), Cerrado (B. paranaensis) and
even AFI areas (B. tomentosa). Barbacenia clade 6 (sister to clades 7–10),
comprising three species, diversified during the Late Miocene, in an
uncertain ancestral area, but the greatest probability being for Chapada
Diamantina. Clade 6 includes a species endemic to Pantepui region
(B. celiae), and this disjunct distribution pattern suggests an event of
long-distance dispersal, as already suggested for other families, as
Araliaceae, Bonnetiaceae, Bromeliaceae, Eriocaulaceae, Fabaceae,
Melastomataceae, Rubiaceae, and Melastomataceae (Fiaschi and Pirani,
2009). Our study thus confirms that floristic exchanges between CR and
AFI have taken place several times independently throughout the
Neogene as suggested by several phylogenetic studies (see references in
the Introduction). However, Barbacenia appears to be rather excep­
tional, because it formed two clades that diversify within CR and AFI and
remained largely endemic to these areas since the Late Miocene in spite
of more recent single colonization events between them. This might be
due to ecological specialization of the species in the Barbacenia AFI clade
to the substrate of these patchily distributed outcrops.
Whereas the definition of the operational areas adopted in the
biogeographic analysis encompasses the whole distribution in Serra do
Mar and Serra da Mantiqueira, they overshadow the endemism of each
recent lineage related to particular mountain areas. There are five AFI
Barbacenia clades with ancestral distribution restricted to the Serra do
Mar or Serra da Mantiqueira, however most of their species occur dis­
junctly in different inselbergs surrounded by a forest matrix (Fig. 1;
Supplementary data 11). The patchy distribution of AFI Barbacenia on
mountaintops may be explained by long-distance dispersal between
these mountaintops or by disruption of ancestral corridors between nonadjacent mountains, formed during climatically suitable time periods
(Flantua et al., 2019; Rahbek et al., 2019). However, for Barbacenia,
ecological preferences for rocky environments could have been a
limiting factor for dispersal along these ancient corridors, and therefore,
stepping-stone dispersal across mountaintops seems to be the most
plausible model to explain its range expansion. Besides, ecological
preferences for rocky environments alongside their geographical isola­
tion, could explain the endemism of several Barbacenia species to single
inselbergs. These factors have also been associated with the long-term
isolation of Sinningieae in these environments (Perret et al., 2012).

and lowland Inselbergs (e.g., Sugar Loaf lands, de Paula et al., 2016,

suggested to provide ecological opportunity for phenotypic diversifica­
tion, perhaps in relation to key innovations that have facilitated adap­
tive radiation (Nürk et al., 2019 and references therein). This might be
the case for the CR Barbacenia clade, where high morphological diversity
does not appear to be a simple consequence of higher species richness.
Both vegetative and reproductive traits might be influential in
facilitating adaptive radiation. In particular in Barbacenia, aquiferous
parenchyma appears to be a key innovation that has triggered diversi­
fication (Alcantara et al., 2018). Other vegetative characters, especially
root specializations, have been suggested to be particularly important as
strategies of Velloziaceae species to live on nutrient poor soils of CR
(Alcantara et al., 2015; Oliveira et al., 2015). Floral attractiveness
related to the flower shape and color suggest different reproductive
strategies, particularly related to the pollination syndrome in the major
Barbacenia clades. CR Barbacenia flowers display short to elongated
hypanthial tube and perianth of different colors (i.e., green, orange,
pink, purple, red, white and yellow), and AFI species, in turn, show
flowers with a short hypanthial tube and a generally purple and pink, or
sometimes reddish and orange, perianth. Whether high morphological
diversity in the CR clade of Barbacenia is the result of adaptive radiation
triggered by a specific driver remains to be shown by studies specifically
addressed at establishing trait-environmental relations during diversi­
fication, needed in an adaptive radiation scenario (Schluter, 2000; Glor,
2010).
AFI Barbacenia clade exhibits a homogeneous vegetative and floral
morphology, and provides an interesting example of long-term
morphological stability. In general, long-term morphological stasis can
be considered paradoxical when compared to events of short time scales
in which organisms adapt to their changing environments (Wake et al.,
1983). Numerous causes have been suggested to explain morphological
stasis, such as generalist ecological strategies, low diversification rates,
stable environments, and evolutionarily limiting biotic interactions
(Zelditch et al., 2020). Lack of change in vegetative morphology might
be due to stable physical environment to which already adapted species
remain well-adapted over time (Ricklefs and Latham, 1992). Stabilizing
selection imposed by the plant-pollinators relationship may have played
an important role in the floral evolution of AFI Barbacenia clade, though
this idea needs to be tested in empirical studies. Similar pattern was
reported for Malpighiaceae and Myrtaceae, in which the long-term
conserved floral morphology was maintained by pollination strategies
that may have reached an adaptive plateau (Davis et al., 2014; Vas­
concelos et al., 2019). The fact that AFI Barbacenia species occupy a
portion of the morphospace nested in the one occupied by CR species,
suggests higher level of specialization in AFI species. Given the habitat
