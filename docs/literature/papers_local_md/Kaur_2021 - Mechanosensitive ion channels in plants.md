16.1 Introduction
All the organisms including prokaryotes and eukaryotes perceive and respond
to various mechanical signals for proper growth and development and stress
(biotic and abiotic) tolerance. However, various intracellular (osmotic pres-
sure) and extracellular (touch, sound, gravity) signals cause plasma membrane
distortion, resulting in the activation of a special class of ion channels. These
ion channels are pore-forming transmembrane proteins, which permit ions to
flow from one side of the membrane to other side down to its electrochemical
gradient. These channels are referred to as mechanosensitive (MS) ion
channels or stretch-activated or force-gated channels. To date, these are best-
studied biological mechanosensing systems that work in response to these
mechanical forces. MS ion channels are ubiquitously distributed in three do-
mains (bacteria, archaea, and eukarya) of life (Kloda and Martinac, 2002) and
showed diversity in terms of their structure, ion selectivity, and physiological
functions (Ranade et al., 2015; Martinac et al., 2014; Basu and Haswell, 2017).
In plant systems, MS ion channels have been playing very significant roles,
from the sensation of mechanical stimuli to the osmotic balance of cellular
organelles. They have also been involved in various plant developmental
processes including pollen tube growth, cell wall damage, plantepathogen
interactions, and lateral root emergence (Table 16.1) (Appel and Cocroft,
2014; Jayaraman et al., 2014; Lucas et al., 2013). The plant MS ion channels
were grouped into five families: MscS-like (MSL), mid1-complementing ac-
tivity (MCA), two-pore potassium (TPK), piezo channel, and reduced
hyperosmolality-induced [Ca2þ] increase (OSCA) (Fig. 16.1, Table 16.1)
(Hamant and Haswell, 2017). The first putative MS ion channel family i.e.,
MSL family exhibited greater similarity with the MS channel of small
conductance (MscS) of Escherichia coli (Hamilton et al., 2015a). This E. coli
MscS showed no ionic specificity and has conductance of about 1-nanoSiemen

(Bagriantsev et al., 2011; Maathuis, 2011). Due to the basal activity of TPK
channels in the absence of membrane tension, they are known as a leaky or
spontaneous channel. The TPK channels were previously described in some
plant species such as A. thaliana, O. sativa, and H. vulgare and act as
osmosensors in tonoplast (Maathuis, 2011).

    The fourth MS channel family i.e., piezo channels were firstly reported in
mouse neuroblastoma cells by Coste et al. (2010). A lot of research work has
been done on piezo channels in the case of animals. While in plants, only one
gene encoding piezo channels proteins was identified in the genome of
A. thaliana (Coste et al., 2010; Kurusu et al., 2013).
    The fifth MS channel family i.e., the hyperosmolality-gated calcium-
permeable channels (OSCA) are only characterized in A. thaliana and work
as an osmosensor that promotes hyperosmolality-induced Ca2þ increases
(Yuan et al., 2014).
    By using patch-clamp electrophysiological approaches, various MS ion
channel activities have been identified, which suggested the valuable contri-
butions of MS ion channels in plant systems. Many other approaches such as
knockdown or overexpression were used for the functional study of MS channel
in plants. Here, we have discussed various roles of MS ion channels in plants.

16.2 Roles of MS channels in plants
16.2.1 Roles of MSL channels
In plants, MSLs are grouped into two main classes, based on sequence
resemblance of their c-terminal transmembrane region and its nearby se-
quences (Haswell, 2007). This region is found to be equivalent to the third
transmembrane helices of MscS of E. coli (Haswell, 2007). The class I MSL
proteins exhibited more sequence resemblance with the bacterial MscS and are
rich in alanine and glycine residues. However, the class II proteins showed
sequence divergence (Pivetti et al., 2003). On the other hand, these two MSL
classes are found to be located in different subcellular organelles. Class I MSL
proteins are reported in mitochondrial and plastid envelope, whereas class II
MSL proteins are in the plasma membrane (Haswell et al., 2008; Saddhe and
Kumar, 2015). These multiple locations of MSL proteins suggested their wide
range of functions within the cells. The genome of A. thaliana has 10 MSL
genes, which were further classified into two classes because of their sequence
similarities and subcellular localization (Haswell, 2007). The MSL2 and
MSL3 of A. thaliana are found to be present in the plastid membrane. In wild-
type (WT) plastids, the rise of membrane tension promotes the opening of the
MSL2 and MSL3 channels, which further facilitates the flow of osmolytes
outside the plastid, hence provides normal shape to them. While in double-
mutant plastids (msl2-1 msl3-1), the efflux of osmolytes becomes difficult,
resulting in the enlargement of plastids (Haswell and Meyerowitz, 2006).
Furthermore, at the time of extreme hypoosmotic shock, msl2-1 msl3-1 mutant
plastids burst inside epidermal cells of leaves, whereas WT plastids remain
unaffected. Thus, MSL2 and MSL3 protect the plastids during normal growth
from high osmotic stress conditions (Haswell and Meyerowitz, 2006).

    The divisions of chloroplasts are essential for maintaining their population
within the cells. The location of filamentous temperature-sensitive Z (FtsZ)
rings determined the site of division and their formation restricted to mid-
chloroplast by the Min system. The live imaging and immunofluorescence
microscopy suggested the presence of many FtsZ rings in enlarged chloro-
plasts of msl2 msl3 double mutants (Wilson et al., 2011). The researcher
suggested that components of the Min system and MSL2 and MSL3 share a
pathway to control chloroplast size and division of FtsZ rings. In addition to it,
the ion homeostasis is balanced by MSL2 and MSL3 in chloroplast stroma;
whereas their absence leads to change in concentration of ion, which further
inactivates the division proteins of chloroplast (Wilson et al., 2011). Moreover,
the knockdown of MSC1 in Chlamydomonas revealed their involvement in the
organization of chloroplasts (Nakayama et al., 2007). In Saccharomyces
pombe, MSY1 and MSY2 were located on the endoplasmic reticulum and
protect yeast cells at the time of hypoosmotic shock (Nakayama et al., 2012).
    In the majority of angiosperms, pollen grains lose water to some extent
before their dispersal and become dehydrated. When the pollen grains meet
the stigma of a female flower, they are rehydrated and reestablish its metabolic
activities (Franchi et al., 2011; Edlund et al., 2004). During pollen grain
germination, an outgrowth termed as a pollen tube is formed, which further
elongates within the pistil and starts moving toward female gametes lying in
the ovary (Wang et al., 2010). The cell walls and pollen membrane face me-
chanical stress at the time of pollen germination and pollen tube growth
(Beauzamy et al., 2014). It has been suggested that MSL8 in A. thaliana re-
sponds to plasma membrane distortion during rehydration of pollen grain and
germination (Hamilton and Haswell, 2017). MSL8 is found to be located in the
plasma membrane and endomembranes of mature pollen grains and then
moved to the germinating tube. Moreover, MSL8 regulates optimal osmotic
potential and prevents the bursting of the nascent pollen tube (Hamilton et al.,
2015b). Hence, MSL8 plays very significant role in the growth of pollen tube.
    MSL1 exhibited electrophysiological similarities with the other homologs
from A. thaliana and E. coli. It is localized to the mitochondrial membrane in
plants and protects E. coli (lacking MS channels) during hypoosmotic shock
(Lee et al., 2016). Besides it, the MSL1 disrupts mitochondrial membrane
potential and promotes the mitochondrial redox homeostasis during abiotic
stress (Lee et al., 2016). According to Lee et al. (2019), MSL1, MSL2, and
MSL3 play a very significant role in interorganellar communication for plant
growth and development. In A. thaliana, MSL10 was present in the plasma
membrane and showed permeability to anions (Maksaev and Haswell, 2012).
Overexpression of MSL10-GFP promotes constitutive production of H2O2,
senescence, ectopic cell death, and induction of genes responsible for ROS
accumulation and abiotic and biotic stress responses (Veley et al., 2014).
Under normal conditions, MSL10 is present in its inactive state (i.e., with a
phosphorylated N-terminus). When membrane tension increases, structural

rearrangement occurs at the C-terminus of the domain, which further causes
conformational changes at the N-terminal domain, resulting in its dephos-
phorylation. This dephosphorylated N-terminal domain triggers cell death
signaling pathways, which are still unknown (Basu et al., 2019). Previous
studies also revealed the involvement of MSL10 in the regulation of primary
events of signaling at the time of wounding. In addition to it, the plasma
membrane and endoplasmic reticulum localized channels of A. thaliana i.e.,
MSL10 and MSL9 performed MS ion channel activity in protoplasts of roots
(Peyronnet et al., 2008). No clear phenotypic changes are observed in single
mutants, the double mutant (msl9-1; msl10-1), and even the quintuple mutant
(msl4-1;msl5-2;msl6-1;msl9-1;msl10-1) (Haswell et al., 2008). Earlier studies
suggested the involvement of MSL4 in triggering immunity in response to
pathogens (Zhang et al., 2017). In rice and common bean, MSL genes
exhibited tissue-specific expressions revealing their involvement in plant
growth and development (Saddhe and Kumar, 2015; de Souza et al., 2016).
However, the majority of OsMSL genes showed expression in the reproductive
stages of rice. The PvMSL1, PvMSL2, and PvMSL3 exhibited higher expres-
sion in leaves and roots, which suggested their major contribution in the
development of these organs. On the other hand, PvMSL6 and PvMSL9
showed higher expression in young trifoliate and nodules (de Souza et al.,
2016). The expression patterns of this MSL suggested their functions in
different plant tissues and organs.

16.2.2 Roles of MCA channels
MCA channels transport Ca2þ in the cytosol in response to mechanical
stresses, hence elevating the Ca2þ concentration in the cytosol. Kurusu et al.
(2012) extracted two calcium-permeable MS channels genes i.e., NtMCA1 and
NtMCA2 from BY-2 cells of N. tabacum. Complementation assay was per-
formed to study the mechanosensing role of those genes in yeast mid1 mutants
lacking MS calcium proteins. Previous studies indicated that when mid1
mutant faces the mating pheromone, it could not survive (Iida et al., 1990;
Kanzaki et al., 1999). However, NtMCA1 and NtMCA2 within the mid1 cells
partially minimize the death of these cells induced due to mating pheromone
(Kurusu et al., 2012).
    During the hypo-osmotic shock assay, there was a rapid increase in the
cytosolic Ca2þ concentration. While overexpression of NtMCA1 and NtMCA2
in yeast mid1 mutant enhanced the Ca2þ intake during hypoosmotic shock.
Hence, NtMCA1 and NtMCA2 participated in calcium influx under me-
chanical stress conditions. In addition to it, NtMCA1 and NtMCA2 promote
Ca2þ uptake in the BY-2 cells of plants. The overexpressing NtMCA1 and
NtMCA2 in BY-2 cells enhances Ca2þ transport in the cytosol, which further
restricts the proliferation of them. Thus, they showed their involvement in the
regulation of cell proliferation.

    Kurusu et al. (2012) further analyzed that the expression of NtMCA2 was
maximum in the G1 phase and minimum in the S phase of the cell cycle. At
the G1 phase, the cells are under the elongation and expansion stage. During
the cell elongation, NtMCA2 plays very important roles in maintaining the
Ca2þ homeostasis at the Hechtian strands and plasma membrane (Kurusu
et al., 2012).
    In Arabidopsis, two MS calcium-permeable channels such as MCA1 and
MCA2 were identified (Nakagawa et al., 2007; Yamanaka et al., 2010).
Subcellular localization studies in A. thaliana root cells show that these
channels are located on the plasma membrane. Earlier studies demonstrate that
the primary root of the mca1-null mutant is unable to penetrate solid agar
(Furuichi et al., 2012). In overexpressing lines of MCA1, the growth of
seedlings gets slower at high Ca2þ concentration but does not affect at its
lower concentration (Nakagawa et al., 2007). The roots of seedling of mca2
null-mutant can sense the hard agar and uptake less calcium as compared to
WT plants (Yamanaka et al., 2010). It is reported that MCA1 and MCA2 play
an important role in calcium influx in yeast mutant cells (Yamanaka et al.,
2010). They are also responsible for the immediate elevation of cytosolic
concentration during cold shock. The cold-induced cytosolic concentration of
Ca2þ is found to be higher in WT (Col-0) as compared to mutants (mca1 and
mca2). On the other hand, double mutant (mca1 mca2) showed freezing and
chilling sensitivity. Thus, MCA1 and MCA2 provide tolerance to plants during
cold stress (Mori et al., 2018).
    In Rice, OsMCA1, a homolog of Arabidopsis MCA1, has been identified
(Kurusu et al., 2012). It is localized in the plasma membrane. Researcher
studied that OsMCA1 mainly expressed in proximal and apical regions of
shoots, mesophyll cells of leaves, seed embryos and roots. Like in Arabi-
dopsis, rice overexpressing lines show more calcium uptake, signifying its role
in calcium influx. Due to overexpression of OsMCA1, activity of NADPH
oxidases increases and shows involvement in hypo-osmotic stress-induced
ROS generation. In plants, OsMCA1 was suppressed resulting into retarded
growth and small rachis of rice plant, indicating its significant role in plant
growth (Kurusu et al., 2012).

16.2.3 Roles of TPK channels
Two-pore potassium channels constitute another MS ion channel family, which
activate under mechanical stress conditions (Maathuis, 2011) and are related to
mammalian TREK Kþchannels of TPK superfamily (Bagriantsev et al., 2011).
These channels are located on the vacuolar membrane in plants and encode for
vacuolar Kþ selective channels (Maathuis, 2011). The activities of TPKs
depend upon the increased concentration of Ca2þ ions in the cytoplasm, but do

not depend upon voltage (Gobert et al., 2007). They are very sensitive to pH
(Gobert et al., 2007) and also modulated by phosphorylation and 14-3-3
protein binding (Latz et al., 2007). Mechanosensitivity of these channels was
studied by the patch-clamp technique (Maathuis, 2011). Various TPKs have
been identified in Arabidopsis, barley, and rice. In Arabidopsis, five genes
encode for TPK channel i.e., AtTPK1, AtTPK2, AtTPK3, AtTPK4, AtTPK5
(Becker et al., 2004). Among these, AtTPK1 was highly expressed and
essential for the closing of stomata and potassium homeostasis in different
tissues. In addition to it, during seed germination, the distribution of minerals
and expanding growth occur via vacuolar potassium ion deposition for the
proper development of radicle. The activity of AtTPK1 is inhibited by Baþ,
tetraethylammonium, and quinine (Gobert et al., 2007). Another channel,
AtTPK4 controls the growth of the pollen tube by regulating the membrane
voltage and potassium homeostasis (Becker et al., 2004).
    Rice genome encodes two types of the two-pore potassium channel, TPKa
and TPKb. Electrophysiological assays depict that these two isoforms show
similarity in their sequences while their membrane targeting was different.
TPKa mainly localized at large lytic vacuole and TPKb localized at protein
storage vacuoles. Both are required to maintain potassium homeostasis
(Isayenkov et al., 2011).
    In Hordeum vulgare, HvTPK1 also involves in maintaining the potassium
homeostasis like AtTPK1 of Arabidopsis (Czempinski et al., 2002; Gobert
et al., 2007). MS activities were predicted in response to trans-tonoplast os-
motic gradients and to the agents that were added in the lipid bilayer. Results
show that AtTPK1 and its homologs in rice and barley have mechanosensi-
tivity. So, they can acts as osmosensors in intact cells (Maathuis, 2011).

16.2.4 Roles of piezo channels
The piezo channels were mainly found in the animals, while their homologs
were found in plants (Coste et al., 2010). One piezo gene i.e., AtPiezo has been
identified in A. thaliana, yet its function as MS channel needs to be determined
(Hedrich, 2012; Kurusu et al., 2013). In 2019, Zhang et al. revealed that the
mutants of AtPiezo, such as esc1-1 (the premature stop mutant), esc1-2 (the T-
DNA inserted line), esc1-3, and esc1-4 (CRISPR/Cas9 mutants) exhibited no
change in inoculated leaves, while in case of systemic leaves, they showed
higher sensitivity to CMV-2aTD2b infection. Therefore, the induction of
AtPiezo after virus infection indicated its involvement in various immunity
pathways against viruses. Indeed, the tissue-specific expression AtPiezo in
vascular bundles suggested their negative on the systemic movement of the
virus. Besides, induction of AtPiezo in petioles of rosette leaves indicated that
it prevents the movement of virus in phloem systems (Zhang et al., 2019).

16.2.5 Roles of OSCA channels
Initially, 15 hyperosmolality-gated calcium-permeable channels (OSCA) were
identified in A. thaliana genome. OSCA1 of Arabidopsis acts as an osmo-
sensor, which is responsible for [Ca2þ]i increases during osmotic stress (Yuan
et al., 2014). In the case of rice, 11 OsOSCA were identified and tissue-specific
expression patterns of these genes determined their roles in the development of
different tissues and organs. Li et al. (2015) demonstrated that they play very
essential roles in the regulation of seed germination, caryopsis development,
and seed imbibitions. They also showed involvement in sensing and
responding to osmotic changes during circadian rhythms. In addition to it, the
expression study of OsOSCAs also suggested their participation in responses to
various abiotic stresses (Li et al., 2015).

16.3 Conclusions
The MS channels play a wide range of functions in plant development pro-
cesses such as pollen tube growth, regulation of plastid shape, seed germi-
nation, imbibitions, and caryopsis development. Besides, they also provide
tolerance to plants from abiotic (hypo-osmotic stress) and biotic stresses (vi-
rus). They accomplished these functions by allowing the flux of ions across the
plasma membrane. The majority of the MS channels facilitate the influx of
Ca2þ in the cytoplasm in response to stress conditions. Until date, MS chan-
nels were recognized and characterized in very few plant species such as
A. thaliana, O. sativa, N. tobaccum, and so on. Various approaches such as
electrophysiology, gene knockout, and overexpression will be used to study
the identification of MS channels and their functions in other plants. The deep
understanding of regulatory mechanisms of MS channels will help us in plant
improvement in the future.
