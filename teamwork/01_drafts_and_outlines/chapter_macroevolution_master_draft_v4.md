## Abstract

Velloziaceae includes species that survive vegetative desiccation and evergreen species that depend on maintaining tissue hydration. How these contrasting strategies relate to genomic divergence remains unresolved. We combined phylogenomics and molecular dating across seven angiosperm genomes with analyses of gene family evolution, local duplication, and coding-sequence divergence, focusing on desiccation-tolerant *Vellozia tubiflora* and evergreen *Vellozia peripherica*. A phylogeny based on 1,513 single-copy orthologs placed the two *Vellozia* species together, sister to *Xerophyta viscosa*. Under the adopted dating model and temporal constraints, their common ancestor with *Xerophyta* dated to approximately 32 million years ago, and the two focal species diverged approximately 10 million years ago. Gene family reconstructions identified expansions before these divergences and contrasting patterns of subsequent turnover. Among 188 families consistently significant across model runs, *V. peripherica* had 72 reconstructed expansions and 61 contractions, compared with 31 expansions and 80 contractions in *V. tubiflora*. Genes classified as tandem or proximal duplicates were also more frequent in *V. peripherica*. Coding sequences were predominantly conserved: among 16,567 focal 1:1 ortholog pairs passing estimation filters, median $d_N/d_S$ was 0.2852 and 233 pairs (1.41%) had ratios above one. These ratios provide exploratory candidates, without statistical confirmation of positive selection. Together, the results describe divergent gene family histories against a background of coding-sequence conservation. They identify changes in gene repertoire as a basis for investigating the contrasting water-use strategies, while leaving the ancestral physiological state and the functions of individual candidates unresolved.

**Keywords:** Velloziaceae; desiccation tolerance; gene family evolution; gene duplication; orthology; coding-sequence divergence.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Water limitation and ecological differentiation in Velloziaceae

The Brazilian *campos rupestres* provide a setting in which the persistence of plants depends on their ability to cope with restricted and uneven access to water and nutrients. Exposed rock surfaces, fractures, and patches of shallow soil create different conditions for rooting and water storage over short distances. Plants occupying this mosaic may experience the same seasonal rainfall regime while differing substantially in the water available to their roots and leaves. The ecological significance of drought therefore depends on both its regional duration and the capacity of individual plants to maintain tissue function as their immediate surroundings dry.

Velloziaceae is particularly informative in this context because its species occupy rocky environments through different combinations of resource acquisition and stress resistance. Specialized roots described in *Barbacenia*, for example, penetrate quartzite and release carboxylates that mobilize phosphorus, showing that establishment on rock can involve active modification of the rooting substrate ([Teodoro et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.13324)). Such adaptations illustrate the close relationship between the physical environment and plant function in the family. They also establish a broader context for interpreting genomic differences: a lineage inhabiting a seasonally dry outcrop responds to several interacting constraints, of which water availability is one.

Within this environmental setting, vegetative desiccation tolerance and the maintenance of hydrated leaves represent contrasting routes to persistence. Comparative work across Velloziaceae has associated these strategies with differences in carbon assimilation and habitat occupancy ([Alcantara et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.12462)). A species capable of surviving extensive tissue drying can persist through periods in which active growth is strongly restricted, whereas a desiccation-sensitive species must maintain water status above damaging thresholds or avoid exposure to those conditions. The distinction concerns the conditions under which tissues remain viable as well as the opportunity to assimilate carbon when water is available.

These alternatives are not equivalent to a simple ranking of resistance. In an experimental comparison involving desiccation-tolerant *Vellozia nivea*, survival under extreme drying was associated with lower productivity under milder water limitation, illustrating how the consequences of a strategy change with drought severity ([Teodoro et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envexpbot.2021.104556)). For comparative genomics, this ecological perspective is useful because it shifts attention from a general capacity to withstand stress to the different biological demands associated with remaining hydrated, surviving drying, and resuming function after rehydration.

The importance of this local environmental variation extends beyond the immediate effects of rainfall. Studies comparing nutrient-acquisition and nutrient-use traits across soils in *campos rupestres* and cerrado have shown that species distributions are associated with the properties of the substrate and the traits through which plants obtain and retain nutrients ([Abrahão et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2745.13111)). Water-use strategies are expressed within that resource environment. For the present comparison, this means that a genomic difference associated with a drought strategy may also participate in growth, resource conservation, or responses to nutrient availability.

This ecological setting gives the focal comparison a more specific meaning than a general contrast between a tolerant and a sensitive plant. The evergreen species must maintain viable tissues as water availability declines, whereas the resurrection strategy includes a transition to a dry state from which recovery remains possible. The duration of each state, the rate at which it is reached, and the timing of renewed water supply may all affect the opportunity for carbon gain. These are relevant dimensions for interpreting genomic capacity, even though they are not physiological variables measured in this chapter.

A useful starting point is therefore to treat both species as successful occupants of a demanding environment. The genomic analysis asks how their repertoires differ in association with those contrasting routes to persistence. It does not assume that a genome with more stress-related copies necessarily belongs to a more drought-resistant plant, or that the strategy permitting survival of the driest tissues is superior under every combination of resource availability and seasonality. This framing links the comparison to ecological differentiation while leaving the direction and magnitude of physiological costs to experiments that measure them.

### 1.2. Cellular protection and the coordination of desiccation responses

Vegetative desiccation tolerance requires the preservation of cellular viability through a sequence of changing conditions. Protection must be established while water is being lost, maintained in the dry state, and coordinated with the restoration of metabolism after water becomes available. This temporal organization makes the phenotype difficult to infer from a list of genes alone. Proteins with protective or regulatory functions may be present in several species, yet their activity may differ among tissues or stages of dehydration. The evolution of tolerance can therefore involve changes in gene number, protein properties, and the regulation of an existing repertoire.

The molecular demands of drying can be understood by considering the structures that must remain recoverable. As cells lose water, macromolecules and membranes experience a physical environment different from that of hydrated tissue. Experiments with late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins have shown that particular representatives can reduce protein aggregation caused by water stress ([Goyal et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1042/BJ20041931)). This provides direct biochemical support for one protective activity. It also indicates why the annotation of a protective protein family is only the beginning of a functional interpretation: protection depends on the protein tested, its molecular partners, and the conditions of the assay.

A study of a mitochondrial LEA protein showed that drying can change protein conformation and reveal protective interactions with membranes ([Tolleter et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1105/tpc.107.050104)). The example connects sequence properties with a function that depends on hydration state. In an evolutionary comparison, an unchanged coding sequence could retain this capacity even if the abundance, targeting, or timing of the protein differed between species. Conversely, an extra annotated copy would not establish that the protein reaches the compartment where protection is required. These possibilities explain why copy number and coding divergence provide different information about the same potential cellular process.

Mechanical responses introduce another level of coordination. In *Craterostigma wilmsii*, analyses of cell-wall composition identified changes during dehydration, supporting the involvement of wall properties in accommodating tissue drying ([Vicré et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0031-9317.2004.0234.x)). This is a physiological problem that cannot be reduced to the activity of soluble protective proteins. A viable dry cell must also preserve an organization from which hydrated structure can be recovered. The example broadens the biological interpretation of a genomic repertoire to include processes affecting cellular architecture, while avoiding an assumption that the same wall modifications occur in the focal *Vellozia* species.

Protection also involves the regulation of oxidative processes. A comparative analysis of antioxidant gene families in resurrection plants combined gene-repertoire information with transcriptome data and identified species differences in the systems associated with reactive oxygen species detoxification during desiccation ([Gupta et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms20123101)). This provides another example in which several molecular arrangements may contribute to a broadly similar physiological requirement. The existence of antioxidant genes is less informative about tolerance than their particular activities, cellular locations, and behavior as tissues lose and regain water.

For comparative interpretation, this distinction argues against defining a desiccation-tolerance repertoire simply by collecting all genes with a stress-related annotation. Many such functions are also required during ordinary growth or other environmental responses. A biologically specific explanation must connect the relevant copies to a physiological state and show how their activity contributes to the outcome of that state. This is why the present chapter concentrates on the observed patterns of family differentiation and conserved coding relationships, while using biochemical and physiological studies to define plausible links between those patterns and survival.

The distinction also helps frame a potential role for expanded regulatory families. A change in signaling or RNA processing could influence several protective processes without increasing the copy number of every downstream component. Conversely, downstream genes could be duplicated while the regulatory apparatus remains largely conserved. These alternatives would produce different combinations of gene-family and coding-sequence patterns. Examining both kinds of genomic evidence therefore provides a broader basis for interpretation than expecting all components of a complex response to show the same evolutionary signature.

These biochemical and structural examples identify distinct demands that a coordinated response may need to meet. A protective component could be present but activated too late, deployed in an unsuitable tissue, or insufficiently integrated with changes elsewhere in the cell. This gives timing a central place in hypotheses about vegetative desiccation tolerance. The relevant evolutionary change might concern the preparation for drying, the maintenance of a dry structure, or the ability to restart a process after rehydration. A comparative genome can identify inherited components and sequence differences, but their contribution must be interpreted within that sequence of physiological states.

The genome of the African resurrection plant *Xerophyta viscosa* provided evidence connecting vegetative desiccation tolerance with the recruitment of programs associated with seed desiccation ([Costa et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nplants.2017.38)). Comparative work in grasses subsequently showed that some molecular responses associated with desiccation also occur during water deficit in sensitive species, emphasizing the need to distinguish a broad drought response from the capacity to survive near-complete drying ([Pardo et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2001928117)). Together, these studies make the regulation of shared functions a relevant component of the evolutionary question.

Gene duplication offers another route by which these functions may diverge. Additional copies can provide opportunities for changes in dosage, expression, or biochemical activity, although their retention does not specify which of those changes occurred. In the comparative system formed by desiccation-tolerant *Lindernia brevidens* and sensitive *L. subracemosa*, gene duplication was examined alongside changes in expression networks, connecting genome structure with the deployment of genes during dehydration and recovery ([VanBuren et al., 2018b](https://doi.org/10.1105/tpc.18.00517)). This combination is informative because a copy-number difference becomes more biologically specific when the behavior of the copies is also known.

For *Vellozia*, these observations motivate complementary questions. The species may differ in the number and identity of retained gene copies, including genes that regulate other cellular processes. They may also differ in the sequences of proteins retained in a reciprocal 1:1 relationship. Neither process excludes the other, and both may operate alongside regulatory changes. A comparison that examines family size and coding-sequence divergence separately can establish which patterns are present before assigning them a role in the physiological contrast.

### 1.3. Comparative genomic perspectives on desiccation tolerance

The accumulation of resurrection-plant genomes has made it possible to compare these alternatives across different genomic backgrounds. The genome of *Boea hygrometrica* combined evidence of historical genome duplication with transcriptional changes during dehydration, including changes in RNA processing and cellular protection ([Xiao et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1505811112)). The assembly of the desiccation-tolerant grass *Oropetium thomaeum* showed that this physiological capacity also occurs in a compact genome ([VanBuren et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature15714)). These observations motivate attention to the identity and deployment of genes rather than overall genome size as an explanation of tolerance.

The comparison extends beyond flowering plants. Genomic analysis of *Selaginella lepidophylla* identified extensive haplotype variation and distinctive tandem-duplication patterns in a desiccation-tolerant lycophyte ([VanBuren et al., 2018a](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-017-02546-5)). Such differences in lineage history make it unlikely that every resurrection plant will display the same genomic signature at the level of chromosome organization or total gene content. Similar physiological outcomes can instead be examined for recurrence at more specific levels, such as the retention of particular functions, duplication of particular families, or coordination of dehydration-responsive genes.

A closer comparative scale is provided by *Sporobolus stapfianus* and *S. pyramidalis*. Their genomic and transcriptomic comparison identified distinct dehydration responses and evidence that the tolerant species was transcriptionally prepared for desiccation before severe drying ([Chávez Montes et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2118886119)). The significance of that example for *Vellozia* is the distinction between a change induced by stress and a pre-existing state that affects the response to it. A genome may contribute to either through its repertoire, but observing gene number alone cannot distinguish them.

These comparisons establish a range of plausible mechanisms for the focal study. They also provide a reason to examine a closely related species contrast within Velloziaceae rather than infer the whole basis of tolerance from a distant resurrection species. A shared genus narrows some aspects of evolutionary divergence, making differences in orthology and family size easier to relate to a common history. It still leaves many biological differences between species, which must be considered when deciding whether a genomic pattern is characteristic of a water-use strategy.

### 1.4. Shared ancestry and the evolution of contrasting strategies

The evolutionary distribution of desiccation tolerance within Velloziaceae raises the question of how much of its molecular basis was inherited from common ancestors. The family includes African and South American lineages with resurrection species, but a shared capacity to recover from drying does not by itself establish a single origin of the phenotype. An ancestral physiological capacity followed by losses and repeated recruitment of shared molecular functions are different historical explanations. Distinguishing them requires information about both the distribution of traits and the branches on which relevant genomic changes occurred.

A dated phylogeny provides the framework for organizing those changes. Broader phylogenetic work in Velloziaceae has examined diversification in relation to geographical distribution and functional traits associated with desiccation tolerance and water storage ([Alcantara et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcy063)). Whole-genome comparisons add information about the genes retained along the sampled branches, allowing an inferred increase in ancestral family size to be distinguished from a terminal expansion. However, a reconstruction of gene number and a reconstruction of a physiological state remain separate analyses. The former can reveal that copies predate a species split without showing that their ancestral expression or function produced the present phenotype.

This distinction is particularly relevant when moving from the African-South American comparison to variation within *Vellozia*. A gene family expanded before the separation of *Xerophyta* and *Vellozia* could subsequently have been retained, contracted, or expanded again in the focal species. Each trajectory produces a different relationship between shared ancestry and present copy number. Examining ancestral and terminal changes within the same analysis therefore helps avoid treating every difference between extant genomes as an independent innovation. It also allows the focal physiological contrast to be interpreted within the longer history of the family.

The phylogenetic structure of Velloziaceae has itself been a subject of investigation using both molecular and morphological evidence. A total-evidence analysis supported a framework of five genera and a sequence of relationships linking *Acanthochlamys*, *Xerophyta*, and the South American lineages ([Mello-Silva et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcr107)). The present sample represents part of that diversity. It includes a comparison between *Xerophyta* and *Vellozia*, but does not include every South American genus or enough species to reconstruct the full pattern of physiological transitions within *Vellozia*.

This distinction between phylogenetic breadth and genomic detail affects the biological questions that can be answered. A broad taxon sample can reveal repeated changes in a character across a tree even when sequence data per species are limited. A smaller set of complete gene repertoires can describe differences in copy number and coding sequence in much greater detail. Neither design automatically supplies the information missing from the other. In this chapter, the genome sample is used to establish a tractable history of the annotated families, while the published phylogenetic context identifies the broader radiation to which that history belongs.

The connection between these levels is especially important for traits that may have changed more than once. If two tolerant species share a gene, their shared ancestry already provides a reason for that similarity. If they share a high copy number, that similarity could represent retention of an earlier expansion or independent increases. A family-size model addresses part of this distinction by reconstructing ancestral counts. Inferring the history of tolerance additionally requires physiological states to be assigned to sampled taxa and analyzed with a model of character change. These are related questions, but their evidence enters at different points.

### 1.5. Gene duplication, retention, and diversification of function

Gene family evolution provides a way to connect genomic variation with the persistence of molecular functions over time. Duplication creates additional copies, but the copies observed in an extant genome have also passed through a history of loss and retention. Comparative analyses of duplicate genes have emphasized that both the origin of new copies and their subsequent evolutionary fate contribute to present family size ([Lynch & Conery, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.290.5494.1151)). Thus, a large family can reflect repeated duplication, sustained retention, or several processes acting over different intervals.

Several outcomes after duplication are relevant to the focal comparison. One copy may retain the ancestral activity while another acquires a different role. Alternatively, ancestral roles can be divided between copies. The duplication-degeneration-complementation model formalized how complementary losses of regulatory subfunctions can preserve both members of a duplicate pair, even without the origin of an entirely new biochemical activity ([Force et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/151.4.1531)). This model makes regulatory partitioning a concrete evolutionary alternative to interpreting every retained duplication as a new molecular function.

In plants, analyses of duplicated genes following polyploidy in *Arabidopsis thaliana* identified differences in retention among functional categories and divergence of expression profiles between copies ([Blanc & Wolfe, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1105/tpc.021410)). These results show that duplicate retention is not uniformly distributed across functions and that sequence ancestry does not ensure identical regulation. They provide a conceptual basis for considering whether a larger family in one *Vellozia* lineage contains copies deployed under different conditions. They do not establish that a polyploid event generated the focal differences, which requires separate historical evidence.

The genomic position of duplicates supplies an additional dimension. Tandem and proximal duplicates occupy nearby positions, whereas other homologous copies may be separated within or between chromosomes. A structural classification uses this arrangement and collinearity to distinguish patterns of duplication, as implemented in MCScanX ([Wang et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkr1293)). These categories summarize the observed organization of the genome. Their biological interpretation becomes stronger when gene trees and comparative positions establish which copies arose before a species split and which are restricted to a terminal lineage.

The relevant unit of interpretation also changes between analyses. A family-size comparison concerns a collection of homologous genes and their counts across taxa. A pairwise coding analysis concerns two sequences assigned to a specific orthology relationship. Copies excluded from a strict focal 1:1 set can still be central to the history of an expanded family. For this reason, the present study does not require a single collection of genes to answer every question. It uses a broad family repertoire to investigate turnover and a separately defined ortholog set to describe divergence among comparable coding sequences.

This division has a biological advantage. It allows a physiological difference to be considered in terms of both changes to the available repertoire and changes to the proteins retained by both species. The same overall phenotype could involve several families that differ in copy number, a small number of coding changes, and regulation of conserved proteins. The aim is to establish the evidence for each genomic pattern before considering how those patterns may interact in the transition from hydrated activity to water limitation and recovery.

### 1.6. The focal comparison and chapter objectives

This chapter focuses on desiccation-tolerant *Vellozia tubiflora* and evergreen, desiccation-sensitive *Vellozia peripherica*. Their contrasting drought strategies are also recognized in a seasonal study of *Vellozia* that included both species ([Pinto et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-025-04984-z)). Here, the evergreen designation describes the focal species' leaf-maintenance strategy; the comparison does not assume that leaf persistence alone determines desiccation sensitivity. The biological interest lies in how two members of the same genus maintain viability under seasonal water limitation through different physiological responses.

We investigate this contrast at two evolutionary scales. Across seven sampled genomes, phylogenomics and molecular dating establish relationships and a temporal framework for reconstructing gene family changes. Within the focal pair, the distribution of local duplicates and divergence among strict 1:1 orthologs describe structural and coding differences more directly. This design connects the history of the broader lineage to the genomic differentiation of the two species without requiring every gene to satisfy the same orthology criterion.

The central question is whether the physiological contrast is accompanied by differences in gene repertoire, changes in shared protein-coding sequences, or a combination of these patterns. We therefore examine ancestral and terminal gene family turnover, compare the contribution of tandem and proximal duplicates to the two annotations, and estimate nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution ratios for reciprocal focal orthologs. The resulting interpretation considers what these observations imply for the maintenance and deployment of shared functions, while distinguishing those implications from mechanisms that require expression or physiological evidence.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Genome sampling and orthology

The comparative design combined a broader phylogenetic sample with a focused comparison of the two *Vellozia* genomes. The broader sample was used to establish relationships and reconstruct gene family histories; the focal comparison examined differences between species with contrasting water-use strategies. This distinction determined which genes entered each analysis. A family could contribute to the gene-count model even when its copy-number history made it unsuitable for a direct 1:1 sequence comparison.

The dataset comprised annotated proteins from four Velloziaceae species, *Vellozia peripherica*, *Vellozia tubiflora*, *Xerophyta viscosa*, and *Acanthochlamys bracteata*, with *Pandanus tectorius*, *Dioscorea alata*, and *Arabidopsis thaliana* providing progressively more distant comparisons. The two focal species were represented by the haplotype A annotations Vper.hapA.v1 and Vtub.hapA.v1. The curated input contained 191,075 protein sequences, using a canonical protein representative per annotated gene.

Orthogroups and orthology relationships were inferred with OrthoFinder v3.1.5 ([Emms & Kelly, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-019-1832-y)), using DIAMOND v2.1.8 in ultra-sensitive mode for sequence similarity searches ([Buchfink et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-021-01101-x)) and a Markov clustering (MCL) inflation parameter of 1.2. Two ortholog sets served different purposes. Phylogenomic inference used genes present in a single copy in all seven species. Coding-sequence comparisons used reciprocal 1:1 relationships specifically between the two *Vellozia* species, with no requirement that the corresponding orthogroup be single-copy across the remaining species.

An orthogroup was interpreted as a collection of genes descending from a common ancestral gene in the species set, while orthology relationships distinguished descendants separated by speciation from copies related through duplication. OrthoFinder uses phylogenetic information to infer those relationships ([Emms & Kelly, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-019-1832-y)). The operational distinction was important because an orthogroup could contain several genes from each species yet still include particular focal relationships that were reciprocal 1:1. Pair inclusion was therefore determined from the focal relationship table, not from the total size of the orthogroup.

### 2.2. Phylogenetic inference

Proteins from 1,513 single-copy orthogroups were aligned separately with MAFFT v7.520 using automatic strategy selection ([Katoh & Standley, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/mst010)). Columns with gaps in more than half of the sequences were removed, and the retained alignments were concatenated into a matrix of 732,066 amino acid positions. We inferred a maximum-likelihood tree in IQ-TREE v2.3.6 with edge-linked partitions corresponding to orthogroups, model selection with ModelFinder ([Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.4285)), and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates ([Minh et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msaa015); [Hoang et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msx281)).

Partitioning by orthogroup allowed the phylogenetic analysis to accommodate differences in substitution patterns among alignments. Bootstrap support was used to evaluate the stability of the inferred relationships under resampling. The resulting topology supplied a common framework for molecular dating and family-size reconstruction, so that ancestral changes and terminal changes referred to the same set of sampled relationships.

### 2.3. Molecular dating

Molecular dating used the fixed maximum-likelihood topology and MCMCtree in PAML v4.10.8 ([Yang, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msm088)), with an independent-rates lognormal clock and approximate likelihood. Amino acid substitution followed a Poisson model with five gamma rate categories and shape parameter 0.5. The birth-death-sampling prior was specified as `1 1 0.1`, with `rgene_gamma = 2 20 1` and `sigma2_gamma = 1 10 1`, in units of 100 million years. The sampling configuration specified 20,000 burn-in iterations and 20,000 retained samples at intervals of ten iterations.

Temporal bounds were 130-145&nbsp;Ma at the root of the sampled tree, 115-128&nbsp;Ma at the *Dioscorea*-Pandanales split, 90-105&nbsp;Ma at the *Pandanus*-Velloziaceae split, 75-90&nbsp;Ma at the split between *Acanthochlamys* and the remaining sampled Velloziaceae, and 2-25&nbsp;Ma at the focal *Vellozia* split. The *Xerophyta*-*Vellozia* node had no direct calibration, but its age remained conditional on the constraints elsewhere in the tree. We report posterior means and associated 95% credible ranges.

The approximate-likelihood approach makes dating large sequence datasets computationally tractable by using information about the likelihood surface for branch lengths rather than recalculating the full sequence likelihood at every sampling step ([dos Reis & Yang, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msr045)). In the recorded workflow, likelihood information was first obtained for the fixed topology and then supplied to the time-estimation stage. Thus, the posterior age estimates combine the molecular branch-length information with assumptions about lineage rates and temporal constraints.

The interpretation of those constraints follows the distinction between molecular information and prior information about time. Bayesian calibration methods allow uncertainty in node ages to be represented by distributions, including bounds that retain some probability outside their nominal interval ([Yang & Rannala, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msj024)). The absence of a direct bound on a particular node does not remove its dependence on neighboring nodes or on the rate model. Accordingly, dates were used here as conditional estimates within the specified framework, rather than as calibration-free observations of historical events.

### 2.4. Gene family evolution

Gene family evolution was modeled with CAFE v5.1.0 using the dated ultrametric tree ([Mendes et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btaa1022)). Of 17,750 orthogroups, 15 containing at least 100 genes in any species were excluded. Filtering for inferred presence at the root yielded 12,276 families for modeling. The primary model used two gamma rate categories and a fitted count-error parameter of 0.006092; a three-category model assessed sensitivity to rate heterogeneity. Family-level probabilities were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure ([Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x)). The consensus set required $q \leq 0.05$ in all five primary-model runs and all four valid sensitivity-model runs. Expansions and contractions refer to reconstructed changes on individual branches within that family-level consensus, rather than separate claims of branch-level false discovery rate control.

Repeated model runs assessed the stability of family-level significance across numerical optimizations and assumptions about rate heterogeneity. They were computational runs of the same biological dataset, not biological replicates. For families in the consensus set, the direction of change on a branch was obtained from reconstructed family sizes under the primary model. The resulting branch summaries could overlap because one family may have changed on several branches.

### 2.5. Local duplication and functional annotation

Duplication modes in the focal genomes were assigned with the MCScanX `duplicate_gene_classifier` ([Wang et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkr1293)), using gene coordinates and the complete within-genome homology inputs, including local duplicate matches. The classification used an E-value threshold of $10^{-5}$, at least five anchors per collinear block, at most 25 intervening genes for block chaining, and a proximal-duplicate distance parameter of ten genes. Tandem and proximal categories were summed and expressed as a proportion of each annotated gene set. Functional descriptions were taken from the existing orthogroup annotation table and interpreted as homology-based assignments.

The structural analysis and the family-size analysis addressed related but distinct properties. Family counts were compared across the phylogeny, whereas duplication categories described the organization of homologous genes within each focal genome. We interpreted agreement between these patterns as complementary evidence about the annotated repertoire. The classification did not supply duplication dates or establish the biochemical activity of individual copies. Functional examples were restricted to families whose current descriptions could be matched to the corresponding counts.

### 2.6. Coding-sequence divergence between the focal species

We retained an ortholog pair only when each member had exactly one partner in the other focal species across the OrthoFinder pairwise relationships. This reciprocal uniqueness criterion excluded focal one-to-many and many-to-many relationships while allowing orthologs and additional copies in other species. It yielded 16,609 pairs across 12,158 orthogroups.

Coding sequences were checked for reading-frame integrity, correspondence with the annotated protein, and internal stop codons. Proteins were aligned globally with Biopython v1.87 ([Cock et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btp163)), using the Gotoh algorithm, BLOSUM62, and gap-opening and gap-extension scores of -10 and -0.5. The complete protein alignment was projected onto the coding sequences before columns containing gaps or invalid codons were removed. At least 30 aligned codons were required. Projecting the alignment before filtering preserved the correspondence between amino acids and their original codons, while removing incomplete columns restricted rate estimation to positions comparable between the two sequences.

Nonsynonymous ($d_N$, or $K_a$) and synonymous ($d_S$, or $K_s$) substitution rates were estimated with the Yang-Nielsen approximate method in `yn00`, PAML v4.10.10 ([Yang & Nielsen, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.molbev.a026236)), using the standard genetic code, `weighting = 0`, and `commonf3x4 = 0`. We calculated $\omega = d_N/d_S$ and retained finite, nonnegative estimates below 99 with $0 < d_S < 2$. Ratios above one were treated as an exploratory screen. No formal test of positive selection was performed, and pairwise estimates were not assigned to either terminal lineage.

The statistical summaries were kept at the level of the analysis that generated them. Family-level significance identified an unusual history of copy-number change across the tree under the fitted birth-and-death model. The inferred sign of change on a branch then described where that history included expansion or contraction. Functional descriptions were used to interpret examples from the resulting set; an annotation shared by several families was not itself treated as evidence of functional enrichment. No enrichment test was performed on the pairwise ratios above one.

The pairwise sequence analysis used an estimation threshold, whereas the family analysis used adjusted probabilities. These criteria should not be read as interchangeable levels of statistical support. A family in the consensus set satisfied the model-based significance criterion across the retained runs. A pair with a ratio above one satisfied a descriptive rate criterion and remained subject to the uncertainty of that estimate. This distinction was retained when integrating the results in the discussion.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Phylogenetic and temporal context

OrthoFinder assigned 178,019 proteins to 17,750 orthogroups, of which 9,826 contained representatives from every sampled species. Among the sampled taxa, the phylogeny inferred from the 1,513 universally single-copy orthogroups recovered the two *Vellozia* species together, with *X. viscosa* as their sister lineage and *A. bracteata* sister to that group. All internal relationships received 100% ultrafast bootstrap support.

Under the adopted dating model, the split between *Acanthochlamys* and the remaining sampled Velloziaceae had a posterior mean of 78.21&nbsp;Ma. The common ancestor of *Xerophyta* and the two *Vellozia* species dated to 31.92&nbsp;Ma, with a reported 95% credible range of 19.26-43.66&nbsp;Ma. The focal *Vellozia* divergence dated to 9.74&nbsp;Ma, with a corresponding range of 5.29-15.03&nbsp;Ma. These estimates place the two focal lineages within a Cenozoic history of divergence, with substantial uncertainty around individual node ages.

### 3.2. Ancestral and terminal changes in gene family size

The consensus analysis identified 188 families with significant family-level change across all retained model runs. Within this set, ancestral reconstructions placed 100 expansions and 17 contractions on the branch leading to the common ancestor of *Xerophyta* and the two *Vellozia* species. Subsequent turnover differed between the focal lineages. *V. peripherica* had more reconstructed expansions than *V. tubiflora*, whereas contractions were more frequent in *V. tubiflora* (Table 1).

The reconstructed directions describe a contrast in family histories within the consensus set. Expansions outnumbered contractions on the ancestral branch and on the terminal branch of *V. peripherica*, whereas contractions outnumbered expansions in *V. tubiflora*. These counts refer to families classified by direction of change; they do not represent the total number of copies gained or lost.

### 3.3. Local duplicates and examples of family differentiation

The structural classification also recovered a larger contribution of local duplicates in *V. peripherica*. Tandem and proximal categories together contained 5,625 genes, or 21.25% of its annotation, compared with 4,078 genes, or 15.96%, in *V. tubiflora*.

Individual families illustrated changes in both directions. A family annotated with a protein kinase domain, OG0000306, contained 11 genes in *V. peripherica* and three in *V. tubiflora*. Conversely, families annotated as a pre-mRNA-splicing factor (OG0000317) and a glycine-rich RNA-binding protein (OG0000332) contained 28 and 25 genes in *V. tubiflora*, compared with five and seven in *V. peripherica*. All three belonged to the consensus set. Their reconstructed terminal changes followed the same directions as the observed contrasts: expansion in *V. peripherica* for the kinase-domain family and expansion in *V. tubiflora* for the two RNA-associated families.

These examples show that the overall asymmetry in expansion counts includes changes favoring either focal species in particular families. The annotation descriptions place them in broad categories of signaling and RNA processing, while their physiological roles remain untested. The copy numbers therefore describe family differentiation rather than measured differences in pathway activity.

### 3.4. Predominant conservation of shared coding sequences

Of the 16,609 strict focal 1:1 pairs, 16,567 passed the estimation filters. Median $d_N$ was 0.0217, median $d_S$ was 0.0784, and median $\omega$ was 0.2852. Ratios were below one for 16,334 pairs, a pattern consistent with predominant purifying constraint on the shared coding sequences.

The remaining 233 pairs had $\omega > 1$, representing 1.41% of retained estimates. This small subset provides candidates for investigating elevated amino acid divergence within a largely conserved coding repertoire.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Placing the focal comparison within the history of Velloziaceae

The phylogenomic reconstruction places the contrast between *V. peripherica* and *V. tubiflora* within the divergence of the sampled African and South American Velloziaceae. The two focal genomes form a clade relative to *Xerophyta* and *Acanthochlamys*, providing a framework in which their differences can be assigned to terminal branches or traced to earlier changes in family size. This ordering matters biologically because present-day copy numbers combine inheritance from common ancestors with subsequent retention, duplication, and loss. A comparison of extant counts alone would not distinguish those contributions.

The molecular dating analysis adds a separation of temporal scales. Under the adopted constraints, the *Xerophyta*-*Vellozia* divergence is substantially older than the split between the two focal species. The resulting Cenozoic estimates are compatible with a biogeographical history involving dispersal after continental separation, but their strongest contribution to this chapter is the temporal framework for comparing gene family trajectories. The age of the internal node is influenced by the constraints imposed elsewhere in the tree, even though it lacks a direct calibration. Consequently, this analysis cannot independently establish the route or direction of dispersal.

The broad credible ranges also warrant a distinction between the age of a lineage and the environmental circumstances of its divergence. The mean age of the focal split falls within the Miocene, but the available estimate does not identify a specific drought episode or change in habitat as the cause of speciation. Establishing that connection would require a more detailed reconstruction of geographical and ecological history. Here, the temporal result supports examining changes accumulated along separated lineages, while the physiological contrast supplies a biological context for interpreting those changes.

The phylogenomic and ecological scales are therefore complementary. Dense taxon sampling is particularly important for reconstructing the distribution of water-use strategies across the family, whereas the present genome sampling provides detailed information about the gene repertoires of a smaller set of species. The 100% bootstrap support in the concatenated analysis establishes strong support for the recovered relationships within that set. It does not extend the focal sister relationship to unsampled species or substitute for an analysis of trait evolution. Maintaining this distinction allows the chapter to use its genomic resolution without assigning it a broader taxonomic scope than the data support.

### 4.2. Ancestral gene family changes and the origin of physiological differences

The 100 reconstructed expansions on the branch ancestral to sampled *Xerophyta* and *Vellozia* indicate that a substantial component of the significant family-size changes preceded the separation of those lineages. These families provide a starting point for examining how inherited copies contributed to later genomic differentiation. A family that expanded ancestrally may subsequently remain large in both focal species, contract in one of them, or undergo additional duplication. The ancestral reconstruction is therefore relevant to the contrast even when the present species differ in physiological strategy.

Its interpretation depends on distinguishing the ancestry of genes from the ancestry of their deployment. If an ancestral expansion increased the number of gene copies encoding components of a cellular process, the descendant lineages could retain those copies while altering their regulation. Alternatively, different copies could be retained in different lineages, with consequences for the timing or specificity of their activity. Both histories are compatible with shared gene ancestry and divergent physiology. The family-size reconstruction does not resolve which occurred, but it identifies branches and families within which those alternatives can be investigated.

The study of *X. viscosa* provides a useful biological comparison because its genomic and transcriptomic evidence connected vegetative tolerance to the recruitment of seed-associated programs ([Costa et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nplants.2017.38)). In the context of the present results, that work motivates a distinction between retaining the molecular components of protection and organizing them into a vegetative response. An ancestral repertoire could provide components used in several physiological settings. Its presence would not imply that the common ancestor expressed those components in leaves during drying or could recover from desiccation.

The hypothesis of ancestral tolerance followed by a loss in *V. peripherica* remains one possible explanation of the focal contrast. Independent acquisition of vegetative tolerance from shared resources, or a more complex history involving repeated gains and losses, could also produce parts of the observed genomic pattern. The current analysis does not compare these physiological histories directly. What it establishes is a gene family history within which such hypotheses must be placed. The 100 expansions should consequently be interpreted as reconstructed changes in gene repertoire, without collectively assigning them a desiccation-protective function.

Evidence from *Xerophyta humilis* makes the distinction between inherited components and inherited regulation more precise. The canonical seed LAFL network comprises LEAFY COTYLEDON 1, ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 3, FUSCA 3, and LEAFY COTYLEDON 2. A study of *X. humilis* did not support simple reactivation of this entire canonical network as the explanation for vegetative desiccation tolerance ([Lyall et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.14596)). This does not negate the relevance of seed-associated protective functions. It shows that recruitment of those functions and reuse of the same upstream regulatory organization are distinct propositions.

For the ancestral expansions in the present study, the implication is that gene-family continuity may coexist with regulatory divergence at several levels. A descendant could retain a protective protein while changing the transcription factor that activates it. Two copies could maintain similar coding sequences but differ in the conditions under which they are expressed. Different tissues could use overlapping components without engaging an identical developmental program. These are evolutionary possibilities consistent with an inherited repertoire, and they explain why family history alone cannot establish the ancestral physiological state.

The ancestral hypothesis can nevertheless be developed into specific expectations. If a functionally relevant expansion occurred before the *Xerophyta*-*Vellozia* split, an investigation of the family should recover copies whose divergence is older than the terminal species separation, subject to later losses. If the present copy numbers instead arose through independent duplication, the gene genealogy should place the corresponding increases within different terminal histories. Those expectations concern the origin of copies. A separate comparison of expression or activity would then be needed to ask whether the copies participate in the same physiological process in each species. This sequence of inference connects ancestral reconstruction to biological mechanism without collapsing the two.

The same reasoning applies to contraction. An inferred reduction in a family can identify a lineage in which fewer copies were retained, but the effect depends on which copies were lost and what the remaining copies do. A retained gene might supply an activity previously distributed among several copies, or the loss might affect only a condition that no longer occurs in the same way. Consequently, neither ancestral expansion nor terminal contraction has an invariant physiological meaning. Their value lies in locating changes in the repertoire that can be interpreted in relation to the biology of the descendants.

### 4.3. Gene repertoire differentiation in the evergreen and desiccation-tolerant lineages

The terminal branches show a consistent asymmetry within the consensus family set: *V. peripherica* has more reconstructed expansions, while *V. tubiflora* has more contractions. The higher proportion of tandem and proximal duplicates in *V. peripherica* adds a structural description to that pattern. Together, the results indicate that the two genomes differ in the extent of family expansion and in the present contribution of local duplicates. These observations support examining copy-number evolution as part of their differentiation, although they do not quantify its causal contribution to water-use strategy.

The possible biological consequences of an additional copy depend on what happens after duplication. Copies can retain similar functions, become expressed under different conditions, or diverge in activity. Family size therefore represents the available genetic repertoire, while expression and protein measurements are needed to determine how that repertoire is used. Local proximity adds information about genomic organization, but does not establish that neighboring copies have the same expression pattern or that they originated after the focal species split. The present results support differences in the retention and organization of duplicates; their individual histories remain to be resolved.

It is also useful to separate the origin of a duplicate from its persistence. Comparative evidence that many duplicate genes are lost after their formation shows why extant family size reflects a history of survival as well as production of copies ([Lynch & Conery, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.290.5494.1151)). In *V. peripherica*, the larger local-duplicate category could therefore include copies retained from earlier events as well as copies formed more recently. Their classification supplies a structural pattern, while their age distribution would require genealogical and positional comparisons that are not provided by the category totals.

A second distinction concerns the scale of the reported asymmetry. The 72 and 31 expansions refer to the number of consensus families with positive reconstructed changes on the two terminal branches. A family gaining one copy and a family gaining several copies each contribute one entry to this count. The structural percentages instead refer to individual genes assigned to tandem or proximal categories across the annotated genomes. Agreement in the direction of these summaries is informative, but their different units prevent a direct conversion of one into the other. The results jointly describe a greater representation of expansion and local duplication in *V. peripherica* without estimating a single rate of adaptive genome enlargement.

Studies of other resurrection plants illustrate why the link between duplication and phenotype is most informative when several forms of evidence are combined. Repeated expansion of early light-induced proteins across desiccation-tolerant land plants has been examined alongside their expression during drying, connecting a recurrent structural pattern with a candidate protective process ([VanBuren et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.18.01420)). This provides a comparative example of how a family expansion can be evaluated biologically. It does not imply that the same family or mechanism explains the greater proportion of local duplicates in *V. peripherica*.

The physiology of desiccation tolerance also argues against equating more gene copies with a universally more demanding strategy. Experimental work in *V. nivea* identified costs to productivity during milder drought alongside survival benefits during extreme drying ([Teodoro et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envexpbot.2021.104556)). Those results concern another species and a physiological experiment, but they show why the costs and benefits of a strategy must be defined relative to environmental conditions. In the focal genomes, counts of genes cannot be converted into estimates of carbon expenditure, metabolic efficiency, or survival.

Both focal genomes thus show family-specific changes that could matter for their respective strategies. More contractions in *V. tubiflora* do not imply a generally simplified stress response, just as more expansions in *V. peripherica* do not establish a higher overall physiological investment. The informative pattern is the composition of the changes: which families differ, how their members are organized, and whether those differences recur in species with similar strategies. The structural and family-size analyses define this pattern at the genomic level and identify where functional comparisons would be most informative.

### 4.4. Signaling and RNA-associated functions as biological candidates

The functional examples are most useful when interpreted as different kinds of regulatory capacity. The kinase-domain family expanded in *V. peripherica* points to proteins with the potential to participate in phosphorylation-dependent processes. The splicing-factor and glycine-rich RNA-binding families expanded in *V. tubiflora* point to processes affecting RNA after it is transcribed. These categories occupy different positions between the perception of an environmental change and the production or activity of downstream proteins. Their coexistence in the comparison suggests several possible routes by which a similar set of cellular functions could be deployed differently.

Experimental work in *Arabidopsis* provides an example of how a kinase can connect signaling to water relations. Genetic disruption of the calcium-dependent protein kinase genes *CPK3* and *CPK6* affected abscisic acid regulation of guard-cell ion channels and stomatal closure ([Mori et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.0040327)). The result establishes a causal connection for those particular proteins in that model. It does not identify OG0000306 as a CPK family or demonstrate an equivalent stomatal role in *V. peripherica*. The relevant comparison is at the level of biological possibility: changes in a signaling repertoire can influence a physiological response through specific substrates and cell types.

For the evergreen species, a signaling hypothesis would concern the coordination of tissue function as water becomes limiting. An additional kinase copy could potentially differ in its expression domain, interaction partners, or response threshold. These alternatives would have different implications. Expression in guard cells would raise questions about water loss through stomata; expression in roots or growing tissues would suggest other links to water acquisition or growth. The current domain-level annotation does not select among these alternatives, but it defines what additional information is needed before a copy-number difference can be connected to leaf maintenance.

The RNA-associated expansions offer a complementary perspective for *V. tubiflora*. Transcript abundance is only one aspect of gene deployment: processing can determine which transcript forms are available, and RNA-binding proteins can affect subsequent steps in RNA use. Genome-wide mapping in *Arabidopsis* documented extensive alternative splicing, including transcript forms relevant to RNA surveillance and regulation ([Filichkin et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.093302.109)). This establishes an additional level at which plants can alter their responses without requiring every regulated gene to acquire a new coding sequence.

The connection between RNA processing and metabolic signaling has also been tested experimentally. Loss of the *Arabidopsis* splicing factor SR45 altered responses to glucose and abscisic acid during seedling development ([Carvalho et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.110.155523)). This example shows how a protein involved in RNA metabolism can affect growth and hormone responses. It is relevant to the interpretation of a broad splicing-factor annotation, while remaining distinct from an orthology assignment: the present data do not establish that OG0000317 encodes SR45 or shares its experimentally measured activities.

Glycine-rich RNA-binding proteins likewise have functions that depend on context. Overexpression and mutant analyses of *Arabidopsis* GRP7 revealed different effects under dehydration, salinity, and freezing conditions, together with a role in stomatal behavior ([Kim et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-313X.2008.03518.x)). These findings are especially useful because they discourage a simple assumption that more copies or more expression of an RNA-binding protein must improve drought tolerance. They support examining when the protein acts, which transcripts it affects, and whether the response is beneficial under the particular stress regime.

In *V. tubiflora*, one hypothesis is that the expanded RNA-associated families contribute to the transition between active tissue, dehydration, and recovery by altering the availability or processing of transcripts. Another is that the copies participate in developmental or environmental responses that are only indirectly related to desiccation. The present results provide the family-size differences that make these hypotheses worth distinguishing. They do not measure alternative splicing, identify RNA targets, or establish changes in transcript stability. Describing the families at the level supported by their annotations therefore preserves both their biological interest and the uncertainty about their mechanism.

The comparison also illustrates why functional categories should not be treated as isolated explanations. A signaling protein could alter the activity of an RNA-associated process, and changes in RNA processing could affect the proteins that regulate stress responses. Such interactions would allow a limited set of changes in upstream or intermediate functions to influence a much larger set of conserved genes. This is a plausible organizing hypothesis for the observed combination of family-specific expansions and coding-sequence conservation. Its evaluation depends on relationships among particular genes and physiological states, rather than on assigning an adaptive role to every member of an expanded category.

### 4.5. Coding-sequence conservation and the scope of the Ka/Ks comparison

The predominance of ratios below one shows that much of the coding repertoire retained in reciprocal 1:1 relationships has accumulated relatively few amino acid changes compared with synonymous changes. This is consistent with continued purifying constraint despite the physiological divergence of the species. Conservation in this setting is biologically informative because contrasting responses to water shortage need not require extensive replacement of protein functions. Similar proteins may participate in different regulatory contexts or be deployed at different stages of stress and recovery.

The interpretation of the distribution depends on the ortholog set used. Requiring a single copy in every sampled genome would limit the focal analysis to genes with a particularly conserved copy-number history across all seven species. The reciprocal criterion used here instead asks whether each gene has one partner in the other *Vellozia*, allowing copies elsewhere in the tree. This extends the comparison to relationships that are suitable for the focal question even when the broader orthogroup has a more complex history. It preserves the meaning of a 1:1 comparison without requiring the whole family to have remained universally single-copy.

At the same time, this criterion creates a necessary boundary between the sequence and duplication analyses. A focal one-to-many relationship is excluded from the pairwise set, although it may be central to the interpretation of an expanded family. The median ratio consequently describes retained 1:1 relationships rather than all proteins encoded by the two genomes. It cannot establish that coding changes are less important than duplication across the whole repertoire. The analyses are complementary because they describe different aspects of divergence, not because their numerical outputs provide a common measure of evolutionary contribution.

The 233 pairs with ratios above one identify the part of this distribution in which amino acid divergence is elevated relative to synonymous divergence. Their significance at this stage is comparative and exploratory. A whole-gene ratio combines information across sites and across the branches connecting the two sequences; it does not locate the changes in a particular lineage or identify the residues responsible for an altered function. Estimation uncertainty is especially relevant when synonymous information is limited. These features explain why the screen provides candidates for closer examination without establishing a count of positively selected genes.

The predominance of constraint and the presence of a small candidate subset can be considered together. The species retain a large set of conserved coding sequences, while a minority of pairs show patterns that warrant more detailed evolutionary interpretation. This combination is compatible with a physiological contrast involving specific coding changes within a conserved background. It is also compatible with an important contribution from regulation. The pairwise analysis establishes the background and the candidate subset; it does not choose between those mechanisms.

The distinction between a gene-wide estimate and a test of episodic selection is important for interpreting both ends of the distribution. Codon models that allow selective pressure to vary among sites and branches were developed to ask whether particular parts of a protein experienced a different regime along specified lineages ([Yang & Nielsen, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.molbev.a004148)). A pairwise ratio averages over those dimensions. A value below one can therefore coexist with changes at a limited number of functionally important sites, while a value above one does not supply a probability that a lineage experienced adaptive evolution.

The quality of the aligned positions also affects the inference. Simulations of branch-site tests showed that insertion, deletion, and alignment errors can generate misleading evidence when nonhomologous positions are compared ([Fletcher & Yang, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msq115)). This study concerned a different inferential procedure from the descriptive estimates used here, but the underlying issue of positional homology is relevant to both. Protein-guided codon alignment and filtering help define comparable sequence positions. They do not make every remaining estimate equally informative, especially when only limited synonymous divergence is available.

Model assumptions provide a separate source of uncertainty. Multinucleotide mutations have been shown to produce false inferences in some tests of lineage-specific positive selection when the fitted model does not accommodate the relevant mutation process ([Venkat et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-018-0584-5)). This finding does not diagnose a particular problem in the focal dataset or establish that its elevated ratios are artifacts. It explains why an evolutionary interpretation must consider how sequence differences arise, in addition to whether the estimated ratio exceeds a threshold.

The practical consequence for the biological synthesis is that the pairwise screen contributes a description of sequence divergence with a defined scope. It supports the conclusion that conserved coding relationships predominate and identifies a smaller set of elevated estimates. Those observations can be related to the family-size results without converting the candidate set into a list of adaptive mechanisms. A detailed explanation of a particular protein would require its sequence changes to be connected to a genealogy, structural or biochemical properties, and a relevant physiological process. The present screen identifies where such an explanation might be sought.

### 4.6. Timing, preparation, and recovery as links between genomic repertoire and physiology

The genomic comparison becomes more biologically informative when the water-use strategies are considered as trajectories rather than static categories. *V. peripherica* and *V. tubiflora* differ in their ability to retain viable vegetative tissues through extensive drying, but each individual passes through changing water availability over time. A gene may affect the onset of a response, its maintenance, or recovery after conditions improve. The same annotated function can have different consequences depending on the stage at which it acts. This temporal perspective provides a way to connect family-specific differences with the large background of conserved coding sequences.

The idea of preparation before severe water loss has empirical support in the comparison of *Sporobolus stapfianus* and *S. pyramidalis*, where transcriptional differences were already evident in hydrated plants and the tolerant species was interpreted as primed for desiccation ([Chávez Montes et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2118886119)). For *Vellozia*, this suggests that a relevant difference might be present before a visible stress phenotype develops. A retained or duplicated gene could contribute to that initial state, but its presence in a genome does not establish that contribution. The distinction is between having a molecular component and having it available at a biologically useful time.

Preparation also changes how response magnitude should be interpreted. A species that undergoes a large transcriptional change after water loss may be responding effectively, responding late, or undergoing damage; the number of changing transcripts alone would not distinguish those possibilities. Conversely, a more limited change could reflect a stable protective state or an inadequate response. Although this chapter contains no expression comparison, the genomic results are often used to motivate one. It is therefore useful to specify that the expected link is between the identity, timing, and physiological context of gene activity, rather than a prediction that one species must always show more activation.

Recovery introduces a related distinction. Survival of dry tissue is expressed through the capacity to resume function after rehydration, so mechanisms that preserve structures must ultimately be compatible with their reuse. Work on the *Boea hygrometrica* genome and dehydration transcriptome implicated cellular protection and changes in RNA processing as part of the response ([Xiao et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1505811112)). In the focal comparison, the RNA-associated expansions in *V. tubiflora* provide one possible entry point for asking how transcript use changes across that transition. The connection remains a biological hypothesis, rather than an inference that the expanded copies are specifically recovery genes.

This interpretation places the conservation result in a constructive role. A protein that participates in metabolism, RNA processing, or cellular maintenance may remain under purifying constraint because its core activity is required in both species. Physiological divergence could then arise through changes in the circumstances under which that activity is used. A duplicated regulator might provide more opportunities for partitioning that use across conditions, while a conserved single-copy gene might respond to different upstream signals. The observed mixture of family-size differences and coding conservation is compatible with this division of roles, although the current data do not demonstrate the regulatory connections.

The temporal framework also clarifies how an evergreen strategy could involve extensive regulation without an origin of novel protective proteins. Leaf maintenance during declining water availability could depend on adjusting existing processes before tissue damage becomes irreversible. In the resurrection strategy, the same broad processes might be coordinated with a transition through a much drier state. These are different physiological uses of a partially shared repertoire. The genomic comparison is relevant because it identifies differences in the components and potential regulatory capacity available to each lineage, while the timing of their activity remains a separate level of investigation.

A useful synthesis therefore distinguishes genomic capacity, molecular deployment, and physiological outcome. Capacity is described here by the genes and copies retained in the assemblies and by their sequence relationships. Deployment concerns where and when those genes produce functional molecules and how their activities interact. Outcome concerns tissue viability, water status, and recovery under a specified environmental regime. The chapter provides evidence primarily for the first level and uses the literature to formulate links to the others. Keeping the levels connected, but distinct, makes the proposed biological explanation both more detailed and more testable.

### 4.7. An integrated interpretation of the physiological contrast

Across the analyses, the most coherent interpretation is that the focal species differ in the composition and potential use of a substantially shared genetic repertoire. The ancestral family changes place part of that repertoire before the sampled lineage divergences. Terminal turnover and local duplication describe subsequent differences in gene number and organization. The ortholog comparison shows that many proteins retained by both species remain constrained. Each observation addresses a different stage or component of genomic differentiation, allowing the physiological contrast to be considered without requiring a single type of molecular change to explain it.

Comparative evidence from *Lindernia* is relevant here because it connected differences in gene retention and duplication with changes in expression networks during dehydration and rehydration ([VanBuren et al., 2018b](https://doi.org/10.1105/tpc.18.00517)). In *Vellozia*, the present structural and sequence results identify the genomic substrate for a comparable question about gene deployment. Regulatory differentiation is a plausible link between conserved proteins and contrasting physiological responses, but it remains an unmeasured component of this chapter. It should therefore be treated as an interpretation to evaluate, not as a result inferred from low coding-sequence divergence alone.

The strength of this interpretation also depends on the distinction between lineage differences and repeated associations with strategy. One focal species represents each physiological state, so any genomic difference may reflect other aspects of their evolutionary or ecological histories. Gene family counts additionally depend on the underlying assemblies and annotations; error-aware models address this source of uncertainty but do not validate every predicted copy ([Han et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/mst100)). These considerations define the scope of the chapter's contribution: a documented comparison of genomic patterns associated with the focal contrast.

Within that scope, the results establish a useful biological foundation. The principal question emerging from the comparison is how the retained and duplicated genes participate in maintaining hydrated tissues in *V. peripherica* and in surviving drying and recovery in *V. tubiflora*. Answering it requires connecting particular copies and conserved orthologs to their activity across physiological states. The present chapter identifies the evolutionary context and genomic differences that make those connections testable, while keeping the shared ancestry of the species visible throughout the interpretation.

The need for broader sampling follows from the structure of comparative evidence. Species share evolutionary history, so observations of similar traits or gene counts cannot be treated as independent replicates simply because they come from different taxa. The phylogenetic comparative framework established by Felsenstein formalized why shared ancestry must be incorporated when evaluating associations among species ([Felsenstein, 1985](https://doi.org/10.1086/284325)). For the present chapter, the consequence is that seven genomes do not constitute seven independent tests of the evergreen-desiccation-tolerant contrast, and the two focal species provide a single detailed comparison.

This does not diminish the usefulness of the comparison; it defines its role in a broader explanation. The focal genomes identify candidate relationships among strategy, family history, and coding conservation. A repeated association would require the corresponding pattern to occur across additional, appropriately sampled changes in strategy. A lineage-specific mechanism could still be biologically important even if it were not repeated elsewhere. Thus, recurrence across species and functional relevance within one species are different dimensions of evidence. The current results provide a basis for examining both without assuming that every useful candidate must be a universal feature of resurrection plants.

The resulting model is one of evolutionary differentiation within a shared set of molecular possibilities. Ancestral gene families constrain and enable what can evolve, terminal changes alter the available copies, and regulation can redistribute their activity among tissues or stages. Coding changes may modify particular proteins within that background. The data do not establish the relative contribution of each process, but they show why an explanation based solely on total copy number or solely on a handful of elevated ratios would be incomplete. Their integration directs attention to how particular histories of retention and divergence connect to the organization of the physiological response.

## 5. Conclusion

The comparison of *V. peripherica* and *V. tubiflora* places contrasting water-use strategies within a history of shared ancestry and subsequent genomic differentiation. Gene family reconstructions identify changes preceding the separation of sampled *Xerophyta* and *Vellozia*, followed by distinct patterns of turnover in the focal lineages. The evergreen species has more reconstructed expansions within the consensus set and a greater proportion of locally duplicated genes. The desiccation-tolerant species also retains prominent expansions in particular families, indicating that the contrast concerns the identity and organization of the repertoire as well as the overall number of changes.

Most reciprocal 1:1 coding sequences show a pattern consistent with purifying constraint, with a smaller subset of elevated ratios providing exploratory candidates. These results support considering variation in gene repertoire alongside conservation of shared proteins when investigating the two strategies. Their biological integration points toward the retention, diversification, and deployment of shared molecular functions as central questions for understanding survival under seasonal water limitation. The chapter establishes the genomic basis for those questions without assigning an ancestral physiological state or a demonstrated adaptive function to individual candidates.
