# CHAPTER 1: GENE-FAMILY TURNOVER ACROSS COMPARATIVE SCALES IN VELLOZIACEAE

## Evidence from Paired *Vellozia* Proteomes and a Seven-Taxon Analysis

## Abstract

The campos rupestres support exceptional plant diversity under persistent nutrient limitation and recurrent water deficit, and Velloziaceae encompass markedly different strategies for persistence in these environments. Comparative genomics can reveal how gene repertoires differ among lineages, but gene-family counts alone cannot establish physiological mechanisms or reconstruct ancestral phenotypes. This chapter integrates a focal three-proteome comparison of *Vellozia tubiflora*, *Vellozia peripherica*, and *Arabidopsis thaliana* with a broader seven-taxon analysis spanning the sampled Velloziaceae and outgroups. In the three-proteome analysis, OrthoFinder assigned 64,380 of 69,772 proteins to 16,953 orthogroups, including 8,541 orthogroups represented in all three taxa and 4,445 single-copy orthogroups. A two-tip CAFE5 Base model evaluated 16,032 families and identified 412 with family-wide significance at the 0.05 threshold. It inferred more increasing families on the *V. peripherica* terminal branch than on the *V. tubiflora* branch, whereas exploratory, substring-based annotation suggested different functional emphases among expanding families. The seven-taxon analysis assigned 160,585 of 168,596 proteins to 16,532 orthogroups and recovered 1,424 single-copy orthogroups represented across all taxa. Its OrthoFinder species tree placed *Xerophyta viscosa* as sister to the sampled *Vellozia* pair. A separate CAFE5 Base-model summary showed heterogeneous family-size change among terminal and internal branches, but its calibration ages were assumed rather than estimated. In particular, node 13 represented the ancestor of the two sampled *Vellozia* species, not the *Xerophyta*–*Vellozia* ancestor. Together, these analyses establish differential gene-family turnover across comparative scales and identify testable functional candidates. They do not demonstrate causal drought adaptation, a Gondwanan origin of desiccation tolerance, secondary loss of that phenotype, or the timing of family-size changes. The results therefore provide a traceable, hypothesis-generating framework for subsequent targeted analyses.

**Keywords:** comparative genomics; gene-family evolution; orthogroups; CAFE5; Velloziaceae; campos rupestres; desiccation tolerance.

# 1. Introduction

The campos rupestres of eastern and central Brazil are ancient montane ecosystems in which exceptional plant richness persists despite severe edaphic constraints. This vegetation meets the criteria of an old, climatically buffered, infertile landscape and contains more than 5,000 plant species—nearly 15% of Brazilian plant diversity—within approximately 0.78% of the country's area (SILVEIRA et al., 2016). The resulting flora cannot be understood as a simple response to drought alone. Nutrient scarcity, shallow substrates, discontinuous rock surfaces, and fine-scale environmental heterogeneity jointly create strong ecological filters. Specialized phosphorus-acquisition strategies illustrate this multidimensional setting: roots studied in two rock-dwelling *Barbacenia* species penetrated quartzite and released carboxylates that mobilized phosphorus, although this mechanism has not been demonstrated directly in the focal species examined here (TEODORO et al., 2019).

Velloziaceae are particularly informative for examining genomic variation within this environmental context because the family includes contrasting water-use and drought-survival strategies. Across sampled Neotropical Velloziaceae, desiccation-tolerant plants sharply reduced metabolism during the dry season and were generally associated with exposed rock, whereas non-desiccation-tolerant plants maintained positive carbon gain and occurred mainly on deeper sandy soils (ALCANTARA et al., 2015). An experimental comparison of two other congeneric species, the desiccation-tolerant *Vellozia nivea* and non-desiccation-tolerant *V. intermedia*, further demonstrated a productivity–survival trade-off: *V. nivea* showed lower productivity under mild drought but survived and recovered from severe drought, whereas *V. intermedia* retained greater productivity under mild drought but did not survive severe desiccation (TEODORO et al., 2021). These studies establish heterogeneity within the family, not a universal physiological profile for every species. More broadly, comparisons across vascular plants also caution against treating desiccation tolerance as a single ecological endpoint because desiccation-tolerant taxa differ in resource-use strategy and water-deficit specialization (BONDI et al., 2023).

This heterogeneity is clearer when drought responses are separated by intensity and duration. A time- and trait-based framework distinguishes drought resistance during declining water availability, when plants may maintain growth or turgor through dehydration avoidance, from drought survival after persistent growth cessation, which may involve dehydration tolerance, desiccation tolerance, water storage, or other strategies (VOLAIRE et al., 2026). Such distinctions prevent genomic differences from being mapped too readily onto binary labels such as “resurrection” and “evergreen.” In this chapter, the contrasting ecological descriptions associated with *Vellozia tubiflora* and *V. peripherica* are therefore treated as working classifications that motivate comparative hypotheses. They are not used as direct evidence of unmeasured stomatal behavior, root depth, tissue water content, carbon assimilation, or recovery kinetics in either focal species.

Research on resurrection plants has revealed multiple genomic routes to survival under extreme water loss. In *Xerophyta viscosa*, dehydration induces transcripts associated with seed desiccation and involves orthologues of the seed regulators ABI3 and ABI5, supporting recruitment of seed-related programs into vegetative tissues. The same study found no extensive genome-wide expansion or contraction, although some stress-associated families had expanded (COSTA et al., 2017). Other lineages display different combinations of genome structure and duplicate retention. *Oropetium thomaeum* has a compact, highly contiguous genome, whereas *Selaginella lepidophylla* lacks evidence of lineage-wide whole-genome duplication and instead exhibits extensive haplotype variation and tandem-duplication patterns (VANBUREN et al., 2015; VANBUREN et al., 2018). In grasses, desiccation tolerance evolved repeatedly through a mixture of shared syntenic components, convergent duplication or expression patterns, pathway-level similarity, and lineage-specific mechanisms (MARKS et al., 2024). These findings make two principles especially important for comparative inference: similar phenotypes need not arise from identical genomic changes, and a change in family size does not by itself establish expression, biochemical activity, ecological benefit, or adaptive causation.

The evolutionary history of Velloziaceae also resists a simple vicariance narrative. A dated family-level analysis recovered *Acanthochlamys bracteata* as sister to the remainder of Velloziaceae, described African Velloziaceae as desiccation tolerant, and documented both desiccation-tolerant and highly drought-resistant non-desiccation-tolerant strategies among South American lineages. It also estimated a Velloziaceae crown age of approximately 75 Ma and younger African and South American stem ages, while noting that the separation of South America and Africa predates 110 Ma. Those estimates contradict an origin based on Gondwanan continental breakup rather than support it (ALCANTARA; REE; MELLO-SILVA, 2018). Consequently, even a well-supported pulse of gene-family change on an internal branch would not determine the ancestral state of desiccation tolerance without an independently coded and fitted trait-evolution model.

Against this background, integrating close and broad comparative scales can distinguish localized repertoire differences from patterns distributed across a larger tree. A focal comparison between two congeneric proteomes offers resolution for identifying specific orthogroups with asymmetric copy numbers. A wider taxonomic panel provides context for asking whether those asymmetries are unusual relative to other terminal and internal branches. The two scales are complementary, but they are not interchangeable: the close comparison has limited power to localize deeper changes, whereas the broad comparison depends more strongly on proteome comparability, phylogenetic support, branch calibration, and consistent functional annotation.

This chapter integrates two existing analytical datasets to characterize gene-family turnover while retaining these inferential boundaries. First, a three-proteome OrthoFinder analysis and a two-tip CAFE5 model were used to describe orthogroup composition, inferred duplications, terminal family-size changes, and exploratory functional enrichments in *V. tubiflora* and *V. peripherica*, with *Arabidopsis thaliana* included in the orthology analysis. Second, a seven-taxon OrthoFinder and CAFE5 analysis placed the focal comparison within a broader sample of Velloziaceae and outgroups. The aims were to quantify the observed orthogroup and branch-level patterns, determine where the two analytical scales converge or differ, and identify biological hypotheses that remain testable without treating copy-number evolution as direct evidence of phenotype history. The central proposition is therefore deliberately limited: differential gene-family turnover provides a comparative map of candidate genomic investment, not proof of the origin or mechanism of drought adaptation.

# 2. Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Comparative datasets and analytical scope

The focal analysis used canonical protein datasets labelled `Vtub.pep`, `Vper.pep`, and `Ath.canonical.pep`, representing *V. tubiflora*, *V. peripherica*, and *A. thaliana*, respectively. The input contained 25,551 *V. tubiflora* proteins, 26,466 *V. peripherica* proteins, and 17,755 *A. thaliana* proteins. Sequencing, de novo assembly, scaffolding, structural annotation, and their quality-control procedures are outside the analytical scope of this chapter and belong to the Appendix. No assembly-completeness, chromosome-level, or cytogenetic claim was used to qualify the comparative results because the corresponding local analytical outputs were not part of the inspected evidence set.

The broader analysis used seven protein files labelled `Abra`, `Ath`, `Dala`, `Ptec`, `Vper`, `Vtub`, and `Xvis`. Within the project, these labels correspond to the intended sampling of *Acanthochlamys bracteata*, *A. thaliana*, *Dioscorea alata*, *Pandanus tectorius*, *V. peripherica*, *V. tubiflora*, and *Xerophyta viscosa*. The seven files contained 168,596 proteins in total. A complete manifest linking every taxon to its accession, release, canonical-isoform procedure, and checksum was not available. The taxonomic and release provenance of this panel must therefore be audited before publication, and comparisons of assignment or turnover are interpreted provisionally with respect to the existing file labels.

## 2.2 Orthogroup inference and species-tree reconstruction

Both comparative datasets were analyzed with OrthoFinder v3.1.5. The recorded configuration used DIAMOND for sequence similarity, FAMSA for multiple-sequence alignment, and FastTree for tree inference. In the focal analysis, the resulting orthogroup statistics, pairwise orthologue totals, rooted species tree, and duplication-event summary were read directly from the current OrthoFinder result directory. Directional orthologue totals were retained in the form produced by OrthoFinder and were not interpreted as counts of unique one-to-one pairs.

In the seven-taxon analysis, OrthoFinder used 1,339 orthogroups in which all sampled taxa were represented by a single-copy gene to construct the species-tree alignment. Overall orthogroup statistics contained 1,424 all-species single-copy orthogroups because the complete single-copy set and the subset used for concatenation are distinct outputs. The resulting topology is described as the OrthoFinder species tree. Internal labels were not treated as bootstrap percentages because no dedicated bootstrap replicate set or explicit support analysis was available. No coalescent analysis, concordance analysis, or molecular-dating output was present.

## 2.3 Focal two-tip CAFE5 analysis

For the focal CAFE5 analysis, only the *V. tubiflora* and *V. peripherica* family counts were retained. Families with more than 100 copies were removed during input preparation, leaving 16,391 families at that stage. CAFE5 subsequently excluded families absent from the root, and 16,032 families were modeled. The two terminal branches were each assigned a length of 1.0 as an arbitrary structural distance. The estimated birth–death parameter is therefore expressed per unit branch length and cannot be converted into change per million years.

The available execution record corresponds to a CAFE5 Base model run and does not document an error model. Global model output, branch changes, inferred root and tip counts, and family-wide probabilities were taken from the Base-model files. Families marked significant in the existing output at a family-wide threshold of 0.05 were counted directly. Numerical increases and decreases reported for each terminal branch include all inferred changes, not only families passing the family-wide significance threshold.

## 2.4 Seven-taxon CAFE5 analysis

The seven-taxon count matrix initially contained 16,532 families. Filtering removed eight families in which at least one taxon had 100 or more copies, leaving 16,524 families; after CAFE5 root-presence filtering, the Base and Gamma family-result files each contained 9,209 modeled families. The supplied ultrametric tree encoded node ages or branch lengths corresponding to assumptions of 12, 75, 85, 95, 115, and 135 units or million years. Because no fossil-calibration analysis, dating script output, or molecular-clock result was available, these values were treated as manually specified calibration assumptions rather than estimated divergence times.

Base and Gamma model outputs were kept separate. The global Base model reported its own likelihood and birth–death parameter, while the Gamma model additionally reported a rate-variation parameter. The existing branch-level summary table was traced to Base-model files: the generating script attempted to find `Base_` filenames in the Gamma directory and then fell back to the Base directory. Accordingly, every branch count reproduced from that table is labelled as Base-model output. The lower negative log likelihood of the Gamma fit was not interpreted as proof of model superiority because no complete, documented model-selection procedure was available.

## 2.5 Exploratory annotation and enrichment

Functional interpretation was restricted to the focal pair because no traceable seven-taxon orthogroup-to-function table or enrichment result was available. For the focal comparison, the local enrichment workflow operated on annotated orthogroups rather than individual genes. Its background comprised 14,210 annotated orthogroups, and its target sets contained 819 orthogroups inferred to increase in *V. tubiflora* and 1,347 inferred to increase in *V. peripherica*. The target counts differ from the total branch increases because only annotated orthogroups entered the functional analysis.

The enrichment workflow assigned curated categories through substring rules applied to the predominant free-text EggNOG description in each orthogroup, then tested category overrepresentation and applied false-discovery-rate correction. This procedure is useful for screening broad annotation patterns, but its categories are partly ad hoc and are not equivalent to ontology-based pathway enrichment or experimental validation. Results with adjusted q-values of 0.05 or lower are therefore described as exploratory enrichment signals. Likewise, the annotation reported for an individual orthogroup is treated as the predominant local description among its members, not as proof that every member has the stated function.

## 2.6 Evidence and interpretation policy

All numerical results in this chapter were taken from the inspected OrthoFinder and CAFE5 outputs. Claims from the source manuscripts were excluded when their result files were absent or when they conflicted with the current outputs. In particular, no empirical synteny result, synonymous/nonsynonymous substitution result, formal divergence-time estimate, ancestral-state reconstruction, or seven-taxon functional enrichment was available. Such analyses are discussed only as limitations or future computational requirements. Literature was used to establish ecological and evolutionary context within the taxonomic boundaries of each study, not to substitute for missing focal-species measurements.

# 3. Results

## 3.1 Orthogroup structure in the focal three-proteome analysis

The focal OrthoFinder analysis included 69,772 proteins from three taxa and assigned 64,380 proteins (92.3%) to 16,953 orthogroups. The remaining 5,392 proteins (7.7%) were unassigned. Among the inferred groups, 8,541 contained representatives from all three taxa, and 4,445 were single-copy orthogroups. The analysis also identified 787 species-specific orthogroups containing 3,673 proteins. Mean and median orthogroup sizes were 3.8 and 3.0 proteins, respectively.

Assignment varied among proteomes but exceeded 86% in each case (Table 1). Of 17,755 *A. thaliana* proteins, 15,412 (86.8%) were assigned to orthogroups and 2,343 remained unassigned. The *V. peripherica* dataset had the largest input, with 25,015 of 26,466 proteins (94.5%) assigned and 1,451 unassigned. In *V. tubiflora*, 23,953 of 25,551 proteins (93.7%) were assigned and 1,598 were unassigned. *A. thaliana* had 557 species-specific orthogroups containing 2,523 proteins, compared with 123 orthogroups and 641 proteins in *V. peripherica* and 107 orthogroups and 509 proteins in *V. tubiflora*.

## 3.2 Focal gene-family turnover and exploratory functional signals

The two-tip CAFE5 Base model evaluated 16,032 families on equal arbitrary branch lengths. Its final negative log likelihood was 31,073, and the estimated global birth–death parameter was 0.074560175552628 per unit branch length after 56 attempted values. Because the tree was not time-calibrated, this parameter does not represent gains or losses per gene per million years. Across all modeled families, CAFE5 inferred increases in 924 families and decreases in 147 families on the *V. tubiflora* branch. It inferred increases in 1,549 families and decreases in 84 families on the *V. peripherica* branch. These totals describe inferred numerical change across all families. Separately, 412 of the 16,032 modeled families were flagged as family-wide significant at the 0.05 threshold.

Selected significant families illustrated that the aggregate asymmetry did not reduce to a single functional direction (Table 2). Several of the largest increases in *V. tubiflora* occurred in orthogroups whose predominant local descriptions referred to chloroplast protein translocation, photosynthetic complexes, or organonitrogen metabolism. For example, OG0000016 increased from an inferred root count of 6 to 61 copies in *V. tubiflora* but decreased to 3 in *V. peripherica*. OG0000036 increased from 6 to 35 copies in *V. tubiflora* and decreased to 5 in *V. peripherica*. Conversely, several large increases in *V. peripherica* occurred in orthogroups predominantly annotated with leucine-rich-repeat proteins, receptor-like kinases, disease-resistance proteins, ubiquitination-associated proteins, or peptidases. OG0000012 increased from 8 inferred root copies to 65 in *V. peripherica* and 13 in *V. tubiflora*, while OG0000021 increased from 8 to 37 copies in *V. peripherica* and 16 in *V. tubiflora*. For every family listed in Table 2, the family p-value was printed as `0` by CAFE5. This notation is retained as a software output category and is not interpreted as a mathematical probability equal to zero.

## 3.3 Orthogroup structure and topology in the seven-taxon panel

The seven-taxon OrthoFinder analysis assigned 160,585 of 168,596 proteins (95.2%) to 16,532 orthogroups, leaving 8,011 proteins (4.8%) unassigned. It recovered 8,011 orthogroups represented in all seven taxa and 1,424 all-species single-copy orthogroups. The dataset also contained 1,540 species-specific orthogroups with 6,203 proteins. Mean and median orthogroup sizes were 9.7 and 8.0 proteins, respectively. Although the shared-orthogroup count and the unassigned-protein count were both 8,011, they refer to different quantities and should not be conflated.

Assignment efficiency ranged from 88.3% in the `Ath` proteome to 99.5% in `Xvis` (Table 4). The `Dala` proteome contributed the largest input and the largest number of species-specific orthogroups, whereas `Ath` had the greatest absolute number of unassigned proteins after `Dala`. These differences may reflect true lineage-specific repertoire, but they may also be influenced by proteome size, annotation quality, isoform selection, and source-release differences. The missing source manifest prevents those contributions from being separated in the current analysis.

## 3.4 Seven-taxon family-size dynamics under assumed calibrations

The seven-taxon CAFE5 outputs differed between the Base and Gamma global fits. The Base model had a negative log likelihood of 83,483.1 and an estimated global birth–death parameter of 0.0022109590091792 after 62 attempted values. It flagged 661 of the 9,209 modeled families as family-wide significant at 0.05. The Gamma model had a lower negative log likelihood of 79,951.4, a global parameter of 0.0028896458243847, and an alpha parameter of 1.05816 after 100 attempted values; it flagged 460 families as family-wide significant. These figures describe distinct model outputs. In the absence of a documented model-comparison procedure, they were not combined, and the difference in negative log likelihood was not treated as a formal selection of the Gamma model.

The available branch summary was generated from the Base model (Table 5). It showed substantial heterogeneity in both the number and direction of family changes. Among the focal terminals, `Vper` had 664 expanding and 225 contracting families, including 209 significant expansions and 39 significant contractions, with a net inferred gain of 1,134 genes. `Vtub` had 415 expanding and 333 contracting families, including 106 significant expansions and 57 significant contractions, with a net gain of 547 genes. `Xvis` had more total expanding families than either focal lineage, with 1,818 expansions, but only 39 were classified as significant in the branch summary. `Dala` had the largest terminal counts, including 3,969 expanded families and a net gain of 9,520 genes, emphasizing that the magnitude of inferred turnover is not uniquely concentrated in the focal lineages.

The node identities materially changed the biological interpretation of this table. Node 13 was the ancestor of `Vper` and `Vtub`, whereas node 12 was the ancestor joining the focal *Vellozia* pair with `Xvis`. Node 11 joined that clade with `Abra`, node 10 joined it with `Ptec`, and node 9 joined it with `Dala`; node 8 was the root with `Ath` and had no modeled change. Thus, the 893 expanded families and 126 significant expansions at node 13 characterize the inferred ancestor of the two sampled *Vellozia* proteomes. They do not represent an ancestral branch shared by *Xerophyta* and *Vellozia*. The corresponding Base-model output for node 12 contained 105 expanded families, including 40 significant expansions.

No functional annotation artifact was available to identify the biological content of the seven-taxon branch changes. Moreover, the assumed tree ages did not result from the OrthoFinder topology or a molecular-dating analysis. The branch summary therefore supports a pattern of heterogeneous family-size turnover across the supplied topology and calibration scheme. It does not demonstrate when those changes occurred in geological time, whether particular changes were adaptive, or whether desiccation tolerance was ancestral, convergent, or secondarily lost.

# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Differential gene-family turnover is the shared cross-scale signal

The focal and seven-taxon analyses converge on one defensible biological pattern: gene-family turnover is asymmetric among sampled lineages. In the three-proteome analysis, *V. peripherica* had more inferred terminal duplications than *V. tubiflora*. In the two-tip CAFE5 model, it also had more families inferred to increase and fewer inferred to decrease. The independent seven-taxon Base summary preserved the same direction for the focal terminals, with 209 significant expansions and 39 significant contractions for `Vper`, compared with 106 and 57 for `Vtub`. This correspondence across analyses is notable because the methods summarize different aspects of evolutionary history: OrthoFinder infers duplications from reconciled gene trees, whereas CAFE5 models changes in family size along a supplied species tree.

The agreement does not, however, convert copy-number asymmetry into evidence of a “genomic cost” of one ecological strategy. More duplication calls or inferred family increases can arise from many processes, including lineage-specific tandem duplication, differential retention, transposable-element activity, assembly or haplotig retention, annotation fragmentation, and family-boundary uncertainty. The seven-taxon context makes this caution concrete. `Dala` and `Xvis` exhibited larger total expansion counts than either focal taxon, and `Abra` showed a net contraction under the same Base model. Turnover is therefore a widespread and heterogeneous property of the sampled tree, not a signature unique to an assumed evergreen or resurrection state.

The two comparative scales nevertheless contribute different forms of resolution. The focal model exposes individual orthogroups with pronounced copy-number contrasts, whereas the seven-taxon model locates family-size changes on additional internal and terminal branches. The broader result also prevents a pairwise asymmetry from being mistaken for a family-wide rule. Conversely, the broader table lacks traceable functional annotation, so it cannot explain which biological processes contribute to its branch counts. The current evidence is strongest when these scales remain linked but analytically distinct: close comparison generates candidate families; broader comparison tests how exceptional the overall turnover pattern appears; neither by itself establishes phenotype causation.

## 4.2 Exploratory functional contrasts generate testable hypotheses

The focal enrichment results suggest different annotation emphases among families inferred to increase. In *V. tubiflora*, selected enriched categories included mobile elements, plastid translation, photosynthetic electron transport and NDH, and chloroplast protein translocation. Several individual families with large copy-number increases also carried predominant descriptions related to chloroplast import or photosynthetic complexes. These observations raise a testable hypothesis that plastid-associated family turnover differs between the focal lineages. They do not show that the added copies are intact, chloroplast-localized, induced by dehydration, or required during rehydration. The chloroplast-translocation category, for example, contained only three target orthogroups. Its 6.51-fold enrichment and q-value of 0.046951 describe overrepresentation in the defined annotation universe, not physiological throughput.

This distinction matters because resurrection plants can manage photosynthetic machinery in different ways. In *Craterostigma pumilum*, younger and older plants differed in their balance between photoprotective energy quenching, D1 repair, and degradation of photosynthetic subunits during dehydration, even within a single species (OUNG et al., 2022). *Xerophyta viscosa* provides direct evidence for recruitment of seed-associated regulatory programs into vegetative dehydration responses, but it did not display wholesale genome-wide family expansion (COSTA et al., 2017). These studies show that dehydration responses involve regulation, protein turnover, developmental state, and lineage history in addition to copy number. The *V. tubiflora* families identified here are therefore candidates for expression, structural, and genomic-context validation rather than evidence of a specific kinetic rehydration mechanism.

In *V. peripherica*, selected enriched categories included cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, UDP-glycosyltransferases, wall-associated receptor kinases, LRR receptor-like kinases, and NB-LRR immune receptors. Large individual increases were likewise observed in orthogroups predominantly annotated as receptor-like kinases, disease-resistance proteins, ubiquitination-associated proteins, and peptidases. These patterns motivate hypotheses about lineage-specific expansion in signalling, defence, and specialized metabolism. Yet neither the local literature nor the current analyses establish that *V. peripherica* maintains hydrated leaves through particular stomatal, root, or osmotic mechanisms. The observed families cannot therefore be explained as a demonstrated requirement for continuous tissue surveillance or pathogen defence.

Comparative physiological studies make such hypotheses plausible at the family level while preserving the taxonomic boundary. Across sampled Neotropical Velloziaceae, non-desiccation-tolerant plants maintained carbon gain under seasonal drought more often than desiccation-tolerant plants (ALCANTARA et al., 2015). In the experimental comparison of *V. nivea* and *V. intermedia*, the non-desiccation-tolerant species combined greater productivity under mild drought with stomatal control, osmotic adjustment, and turgor maintenance, whereas the desiccation-tolerant species survived more extreme dehydration (TEODORO et al., 2021). These findings justify asking whether receptor, metabolic, or defence-family turnover contributes to differences among focal *Vellozia* species. They do not permit the mechanisms measured in *V. intermedia* to be assigned to *V. peripherica*.

The same caution applies to apparently coherent annotations. P450s, glycosyltransferases, LRR-containing proteins, and ubiquitination components are large and functionally diverse families. An enrichment category created by a text substring may combine proteins that differ in substrate, cellular location, expression, and evolutionary origin. Some large families are also particularly susceptible to annotation fragmentation or allelic redundancy. Accordingly, the next useful step is not to elaborate an adaptive narrative from these labels, but to test copy-number robustness, genomic clustering, domain architecture, orthology, and expression for selected families. Until those checks are performed, the functional contrast remains a structured set of candidate associations.

## 4.3 The broader analysis does not reconstruct the origin of desiccation tolerance

The corrected node map removes the principal basis for the previous Gondwanan interpretation. Node 13 is the inferred ancestor of *V. peripherica* and *V. tubiflora*, not the common ancestor of *Xerophyta* and *Vellozia*. The branch uniting `Xvis` with the focal pair is node 12, which has a different Base-model profile. More fundamentally, neither node identifies a phenotype state because no trait matrix or ancestral-state model was fitted. Gene-family changes on an internal branch may predate, follow, or be unrelated to the origin of a complex physiological phenotype.

The chronological premise of the vicariance argument is also unsupported. The ages in the CAFE5 tree were manually assigned, not inferred from sequence data and fossil calibrations. Independent literature places the separation of South America and Africa before 110 Ma and estimates the Velloziaceae crown at approximately 75 Ma, with African and South American stems younger still; those estimates explicitly contradict a Gondwanan-vicariance origin for the family (ALCANTARA; REE; MELLO-SILVA, 2018). A model that assumes a 75-unit node cannot be cited as new evidence for that node's age, and an assumed age younger than continental separation cannot demonstrate pre-breakup ancestry.

Available comparative research supports keeping multiple evolutionary explanations open. Desiccation tolerance in grasses evolved repeatedly, with shared genes, convergent genomic patterns, pathway-level similarity, and lineage-specific components all contributing to the phenotype (MARKS et al., 2024). Ecological comparisons likewise show that desiccation-tolerant vascular plants are functionally heterogeneous rather than confined to one resource-use strategy (BONDI et al., 2023). Within Velloziaceae, dated phylogenetic analyses have identified both desiccation-tolerant and non-desiccation-tolerant strategies among South American lineages (ALCANTARA; REE; MELLO-SILVA, 2018). The present family-size data are compatible with several histories, including repeated recruitment, retention of some ancestral components combined with lineage-specific modification, or changes unrelated to drought phenotype. They cannot discriminate among these alternatives.

Claims that *V. peripherica* secondarily lost desiccation tolerance are therefore premature. Demonstrating secondary loss would require reliable species-level phenotype coding, sufficiently dense taxonomic sampling, a phylogeny with documented support and time uncertainty, and explicit ancestral-state reconstruction. Linking that transition to gene families would further require traceable functional annotations and branch-aware tests that connect family histories with phenotype transitions. The current analysis supplies neither the trait reconstruction nor the functional bridge. Its contribution is narrower but still valuable: it identifies where inferred family-size changes occur in the sampled tree and clarifies which nodes warrant future testing.

## 4.4 Analytical limitations and computational priorities

Several limitations constrain the present interpretation. First, the focal CAFE5 model contains only two terminal taxa on arbitrary equal branch lengths. It cannot distinguish deeper ancestral change from terminal change, and its global parameter has no temporal unit. The broader CAFE5 analysis adds internal branches, but its assumed calibration values are not estimates and its Base and Gamma outputs have not been subjected to a documented model-comparison workflow. In neither analysis was an error model recorded. Because family-size methods can be sensitive to annotation error, these issues directly affect the confidence placed in both branch counts and significance classifications.

Second, the seven-taxon proteome panel lacks a complete source and checksum manifest. The strong variation in proteome size and species-specific orthogroup counts could reflect biological history, technical differences, or both. A publication-ready revision should document accession, release, source assembly, isoform-selection rule, protein count, and checksum for every input. It should then audit gene-model completeness and likely haplotig or fragmentation effects before interpreting unusually high turnover. This is especially important for branches such as `Dala`, which had the largest proteome and the largest Base-model expansion counts.

Third, phylogenetic uncertainty has not been quantified. The OrthoFinder tree provides a useful working topology, but internal labels cannot be described as bootstrap support, and no gene-tree concordance or coalescent analysis is available. A dedicated phylogenomic analysis using the verified single-copy set should evaluate branch support, gene-tree conflict, sensitivity to alignment and model choice, and alternative taxon placements. A separate, fossil-informed molecular-dating workflow should estimate divergence times with uncertainty rather than transfer point assumptions into the CAFE tree.

Fourth, functional inference is uneven across scales. The focal enrichment is transparent enough to audit but relies on substring categories and a predominant free-text description per orthogroup. Reanalysis with predefined ontologies, an explicitly documented background, and family-level gene lists would reduce category ambiguity. The seven-taxon branch counts have no corresponding traceable annotation artifact and therefore cannot support claims about LEA proteins, F-box proteins, receptor-like kinases, NLRs, PPR proteins, P450s, or any other functional class. Producing a branch-specific gene and orthogroup ledger is a prerequisite for biological discussion of those changes.

Finally, two major analytical themes in the source drafts could not be retained. The inspected synteny directory contained input files but no collinearity or block outputs, so claims of conserved blocks, absent translocations, or paleoduplication are not supported here. No Ka/Ks or other selection-analysis output was present, precluding claims about genome-wide purifying selection, functional gradients in evolutionary rate, or positive selection. These are recoverable dry-lab gaps. Synteny would require reproducible anchor and collinearity outputs followed, where appropriate, by syntenic-depth and Ks analyses; selection inference would require validated orthologue alignments, codon-aware quality control, and models appropriate to the evolutionary question. Until those outputs are registered, neither result should be anticipated in the chapter.

Taken together, these limitations change the strength, not the usefulness, of the chapter. A conservative account of differential turnover is more informative than a mechanistically rich but untraceable narrative because it identifies exactly which hypotheses are supported, which remain plausible, and which analyses can discriminate among alternatives. It also protects the distinction between comparative association and adaptation. Gene-family changes become biologically persuasive when independent evidence connects copy number to gene integrity, expression, localization, biochemical activity, phenotype, and fitness; the current work establishes the comparative starting point for that chain of evidence.

# 5. Conclusions

The integrated analyses reveal differential gene-family turnover at two complementary comparative scales. In the focal three-proteome analysis, the two sampled *Vellozia* proteomes shared a substantial orthogroup core but differed in terminal duplication inferences and CAFE5 family-size changes. *V. peripherica* showed more inferred terminal duplications and more increasing families than *V. tubiflora*, while exploratory annotation highlighted different candidate functional emphases. In the seven-taxon panel, orthogroup assignment remained high, the OrthoFinder topology placed `Xvis` as sister to the focal *Vellozia* pair, and the Base-model branch summary demonstrated that large expansion and contraction profiles occur throughout the sampled tree.

These observations support a hypothesis-generating model of lineage-dependent repertoire evolution. They do not establish that copy-number change caused contrasting drought strategies, that desiccation tolerance originated before Gondwanan breakup, or that an evergreen condition arose through secondary loss. The corrected CAFE5 node identities, the assumed rather than estimated calibration ages, the absence of ancestral-state reconstruction, and the lack of traceable seven-taxon functional annotation rule out those stronger conclusions. Within those boundaries, the chapter provides a coherent comparative foundation for validating specific families and testing their relationship to drought-response phenotypes. A targeted analysis of the OSCA family is reserved for Chapter 2.
